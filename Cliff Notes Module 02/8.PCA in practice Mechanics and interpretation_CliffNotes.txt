2. THE DATA, AND A FIRST DOSE OF REALITY
  - The Palmer Penguins data set (Horst, Hill and Gorman 2020):
    real measurements of 344 penguins, done by hand, with a
    GitHub link in the notebook. Not the 2-D data set from Videos
    1 and 2 but a four-dimensional one: bill length, bill depth,
    flipper length, and body mass.
  - Real data is rarely clean. A couple of penguins are missing
    one or more of these measurements. PCA needs complete rows,
    so the first practical step is .dropna() on those four
    columns, and the notebook prints how many rows that costs.
    The professor's count: 344 rows before, and dropping the NAs
    removes two.
  - KEY TERM: NA / dropna: NA is a missing value in a pandas
    DataFrame. DataFrame.dropna(subset=cols) removes every row
    that has a missing value in any of the listed columns (added
    definition).

3. THE THREE-STEP RECIPE
  - PCA in practice is three steps:
      Step 1. Standardize the features. Put them all on the same
        scale.
      Step 2. Find the components. The principal directions,
        Video 2's eigenvectors of the covariance matrix.
      Step 3. Project the data onto the top few components.
  - Step 1 is why Video 2 flagged "scaling changes the answer."
    The dimensions potentially have completely different scales
    (millimeters versus grams). Without standardization the
    largest-numbered feature dominates the variance for no good
    reason, and you are comparing apples to oranges when you
    evaluate correlations. Standardization has its own earlier
    video in this module. Here it is just applied.
  - The obvious question about step 3, "how do we find the top
    few?", is answered in section 7.

4. STEP 1: STANDARDIZE
  - KEY TERM: StandardScaler: the scikit-learn class that
    rescales each feature to mean 0 and standard deviation 1
    (subtract the feature's mean, divide by its standard
    deviation) (added definition of the mechanics).
  - After this, "one unit" means the same thing on every axis,
    namely one standard deviation, so no feature dominates just
    because its numbers happen to be large.
  - The notebook confirms it did what it claims by printing each
    feature's mean and standard deviation before and after. After
    standardization the mean of everything is 0 and the standard
    deviation of every variable is 1.0.
  - Usage pattern: scaler = StandardScaler(), then
    X_std = scaler.fit_transform(df[feats]). fit learns the means
    and standard deviations, transform applies them, and
    fit_transform does both in one call (added explanation).

5. STEP 2: FIT PCA
  - Fit PCA on the standardized data, asking for all four
    components for now: pca = PCA(), then pca.fit(X_std).
  - There is no new mathematics hiding in scikit-learn. Under the
    hood:
      pca.components_ stores the eigenvectors of the standardized
        covariance matrix, the principal directions from Video 2,
        now in 4-D.
      pca.explained_variance_ stores the matching eigenvalues,
        the variance along each direction. This is the lambda
        from C v = lambda v.
      pca.n_components_ reports how many components were kept.
        With PCA() and four features it is 4.
  - The one interesting convenience: scikit-learn returns them
    already sorted largest first. The biggest principal component
    comes first, then the second biggest, and so on. That is the
    PC1, PC2 ordering we wanted, and it comes for free.
  - KEY TERM: fit: the scikit-learn verb for "learn from this
    data." For PCA, fit computes the covariance of the input and
    its eigen-decomposition and stores the results in the
    attributes ending in an underscore (added definition). The
    trailing underscore is the scikit-learn convention for
    "this exists only after fit."

6. THE PAYOFF: EXPLAINED VARIANCE RATIO
  - Each eigenvalue is the variance along its component. Divide
    each by the total variance and you get
    pca.explained_variance_ratio_.
  - KEY TERM: explained variance ratio: the fraction of the
    data's total spread that each principal component captures.
    Nothing more than the eigenvalues from Video 2, normalized to
    sum to 1.
  - Plotted for all four components it shows at a glance how the
    information is distributed. See the bar chart from cell 11 of
    8.1.pca_in_practice.ipynb. Bars are each component's own
    share. The green line is the cumulative variance explained.
  - The professor's penguin numbers:
      PC1: 68.8 percent
      PC2: 19.3 percent
      PC3: 9.1 percent
      PC4: 2.7 percent
    Cumulative: two components reach 88 percent, three reach 97,
    four reach 100.
  - KEY TERM: cumulative variance explained: the running sum of
    the ratios, answering "how much do the first k components
    keep?" (added definition). The last value is always 1 (100
    percent) when all components are kept, since the eigenvalues
    sum to the total variance.

7. SO HOW MANY COMPONENTS DO WE KEEP?
  - The professor's pause point: is one enough? Two? Three? All
    four? Read it straight off the bars.
  - PC1 and PC2 together capture the great majority of the
    variance. The cumulative line is already near the top by the
    second component. PC3 and PC4 are the small, barely-varying
    directions, the same situation Video 2 previewed when PC2 came
    out tiny on the two-feature slice, now in four dimensions.
  - Keeping the top two buys a picture you can actually see while
    giving up very little. Two is the answer here.
  - There are more principled ways to choose the number of
    components, a scree plot and explicit cutoffs, covered in the
    next video. For now, two is plainly enough for a first look.

8. STEP 3: PROJECT ONTO THE TOP TWO, AND LOOK
  - Transform the four standardized features down to their first
    two principal components: pca2 = PCA(n_components=2), then
    scores = pca2.fit_transform(X_std).
  - KEY TERM: scores: the coordinates of each row in the new
    principal component axes (added definition). The scores array
    has one row per penguin and one column per kept component, so
    here it is 342 rows by 2 columns. Each score is the dot
    product of the standardized row with one eigenvector, the
    projection from Video 1.
  - The result: four measurements compressed into one honest 2-D
    picture, built from the numbers alone. See the scatterplot
    from cell 14 of 8.1.pca_in_practice.ipynb, PC1 on x, PC2 on
    y. The professor's remark on it: the axes are normalized,
    since the inputs had mean zero and standard deviation one
    before they were combined and projected.
  - This is not "we picked a feature." We picked principal
    components, which are combinations of the different features.
  - The plot has no labels. The original data had three kinds of
    penguins, but everything is gray here. Nothing about species
    was used anywhere in the recipe. What the labels reveal, and
    what PC1 and PC2 should mean, is the next video.

9. THE RECIPE ON A DIFFERENT DATA SET
  - An example. The wine data set that ships with scikit-learn:
    178 wines, 13 chemical measurements, no missing values. Five
    features chosen for the example: alcohol, malic acid,
    flavanoids, color intensity, and proline. Their raw standard
    deviations run from 0.81 (alcohol) to 314.91 (proline), so
    this is mixed-scale data of exactly the kind the recipe is
    for.
  - Code, all three steps:

      import numpy as np
      from sklearn.datasets import load_wine
      from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
      from sklearn.decomposition import PCA

      feats = ['alcohol', 'malic_acid', 'flavanoids', 'color_intensity', 'proline']
      df_w = load_wine(as_frame=True).frame
      X_std = StandardScaler().fit_transform(df_w[feats]) ## step 1
      pca = PCA().fit(X_std) ## step 2, all components
      print(pca.explained_variance_.round(3), pca.explained_variance_ratio_.round(3),
            np.cumsum(pca.explained_variance_ratio_).round(3), sep='\n')

      scores = PCA(n_components=2).fit_transform(X_std) ## step 3
      print(scores.shape, np.corrcoef(scores.T)[0, 1].round(6))

    Output:

      [2.156 1.65  0.607 0.337 0.279]
      [0.429 0.328 0.121 0.067 0.056]
      [0.429 0.757 0.877 0.944 1.   ]
      (178, 2) 0.0

  - Reading it. The eigenvalues (explained_variance_) are 2.156,
    1.650, 0.607, 0.337, 0.279, already sorted largest first. They
    sum to 5.028 (unrounded), which is the total variance of five
    standardized features (each has variance 1 in the population
    form, so about 5, with the small excess coming from the n - 1
    denominator).
    Divide each by that sum and you get the ratios: 42.9, 32.8,
    12.1, 6.7, 5.6 percent. Cumulative: two components keep 75.7
    percent, three keep 87.7, four keep 94.4.
  - The scores come out 178 rows by 2 columns, and the
    correlation between the PC1 and PC2 columns is 0.0. That is
    the decorrelation payoff from Video 3, visible in a number.
  - Independent check on the eigenvalues (added verification):
    np.linalg.eigvalsh(np.cov(X_std.T)) returns the same five
    values, in ascending order. scikit-learn's explained_variance_
    uses the same n - 1 denominator that np.cov uses by default.
  - This data set is less compressible than the penguins. Two
    components hold 76 percent here versus 88 percent there. That
    is not a failure of PCA. It means these five wine features
    overlap less than the four penguin measurements do.

10. WHAT HAPPENS IF YOU SKIP STEP 1
  - The same five wine features fed to PCA without
    standardization:

      import numpy as np
      from sklearn.datasets import load_wine
      from sklearn.decomposition import PCA

      feats = ['alcohol', 'malic_acid', 'flavanoids', 'color_intensity', 'proline']
      df_w = load_wine(as_frame=True).frame
      pca_raw = PCA().fit(df_w[feats].to_numpy()) ## no scaling on purpose
      print(df_w[feats].var().round(1).to_dict())
      print(pca_raw.explained_variance_ratio_.round(4), pca_raw.components_[0].round(3))

    Output:

      {'alcohol': 0.7, 'malic_acid': 1.2, 'flavanoids': 1.0, 'color_intensity': 5.4, 'proline': 99166.7}
      [9.999e-01 1.000e-04 0.000e+00 0.000e+00 0.000e+00] [ 0.002 -0.001  0.002  0.002  1.   ]

  - Proline is measured in the hundreds and thousands, so its
    raw variance is 99,166.7 against 0.7 to 5.4 for the others.
    PC1 now explains 99.99 percent of the variance and its
    direction is (0.002, -0.001, 0.002, 0.002, 1.000): it is the
    proline axis, full stop. PCA did not find structure. It found
    the feature with the biggest numbers. That is the "apples to
    oranges" problem the professor describes, and it is the whole
    reason step 1 exists.

12. KEY TAKEAWAYS
  - Three steps: standardize, fit PCA to find the components,
    project onto the top few.
  - StandardScaler gives every feature mean 0 and standard
    deviation 1, so no feature wins by having big numbers.
  - pca.components_ = eigenvectors. pca.explained_variance_ =
    eigenvalues. Sorted largest first for free.
  - explained_variance_ratio_ = each eigenvalue over the total.
    The cumulative sum answers "how much do the first k keep?"
  - Penguins: 68.8, 19.3, 9.1, 2.7 percent. Two components keep
    88 percent, so keep two.
  - PCA(n_components=2).fit_transform(X_std) gives the scores,
    one row per penguin, two columns.
  - The new axes are blends of features, not features. No labels
    were used.

13. QUIZ-SAFE FACTS
  - The Palmer Penguins data: 344 penguins, four numeric
    features: bill length, bill depth, flipper length, body mass.
    NAs are dropped before PCA.
  - Imports: StandardScaler from sklearn.preprocessing, PCA from
    sklearn.decomposition.
  - PCA in practice is three steps: standardize, find the
    components, project onto the top few.
  - Standardize first because features on different scales
    distort the covariance and the largest-numbered feature would
    dominate.
  - After StandardScaler, every feature has mean 0 and standard
    deviation 1.
  - pca.components_ holds the eigenvectors (principal
    directions). pca.explained_variance_ holds the eigenvalues
    (variance along each direction, lambda).
  - scikit-learn returns components sorted largest first.
  - explained_variance_ratio_ is each eigenvalue divided by the
    total variance, the fraction of spread each component
    captures. The ratios sum to 1.
  - Penguin results: PC1 68.8 percent, PC2 19.3, PC3 9.1, PC4
    2.7. Cumulative: 88 percent with two, 97 with three, 100
    with four.
  - The professor keeps two components: the vast majority of the
    variance, and a picture you can see.
  - Projection to two components: PCA(n_components=2) and
    fit_transform on the standardized data.
  - Principal components are combinations of the features, not
    single features picked out.
  - The 2-D projection used no species labels. Labels come in the
    next video, along with loadings and the scree plot.
