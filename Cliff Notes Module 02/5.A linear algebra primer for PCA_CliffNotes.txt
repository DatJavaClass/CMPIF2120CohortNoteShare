1. WHAT THIS VIDEO IS FOR
  - First of a five video series on principal component analysis
    (PCA). Textbook treatments of PCA are heavy on math. This
    series builds the intuition through linear algebra
    fundamentals, visuals on real data, and a slow pace.
  - The notebook (5.1.geometry_of_data.ipynb) is mostly plotting
    code. Every code cell carries a note that it exists only to
    generate the figure and that understanding it is not
    required. The concepts are the deliverable, not the code.
  - KEY TERM: principal component analysis (PCA): a method that
    finds the directions along which a dataset varies the most,
    so the data can be described with fewer numbers while losing
    as little information as possible. Defined properly in the
    later videos. (added definition)
  - KEY TERM: linear algebra: the math of vectors and matrices,
    which is to say the math of tables of numbers and the
    arrows they can be drawn as. (added definition)
  - The thread to follow through this whole note (added): a row
    of data is a point, a point is an arrow, an arrow can be
    measured along any direction, and some directions capture
    more of the data's spread than others. PCA is about finding
    those directions. This video gets you to "some directions
    are better" by hand. Video 2 automates it.

2. A DATA POINT IS A THING WITH MEASUREMENTS
  - In applied predictive modeling, a data point is just some
    measurements of a real object. One, two, or more attributes.
  - The warm-up data is vehicles with two measurements, length in
    meters and weight in kilograms. First a small set (sedan,
    SUV, pickup truck, delivery van), then the full list of eight
    vehicles (Smart Fortwo, Mini Cooper, compact hatchback,
    sedan, SUV, minivan, pickup truck, delivery van). Real
    vehicles, representative numbers, not a specific brand.
  - The scatter plot in cell 5 of the notebook puts length on the
    x-axis and weight on the y-axis. Each dot is one vehicle.
    Cell 7 highlights the two extremes: the Smart Fortwo at the
    bottom left (2.7 m, 900 kg) and the delivery van at the top
    right (6.5 m, 3,500 kg).
  - The notebook's phrasing for the key idea: a row of numbers
    and a point in space are the same information in two forms.
  - KEY TERM: data point: one record, one object, one row of the
    table, drawn as one dot. (added definition)

3. A POINT IS ALSO AN ARROW: VECTORS
  - KEY TERM: vector: an arrow from the origin (0, 0) out to the
    point. A vector has a direction and a length, also called
    magnitude.
  - KEY TERM: origin: the point (0, 0), where every measurement
    is zero. (added definition)
  - For the vehicles the origin is a natural starting point: no
    length, no weight. Cell 9 of the notebook draws the arrows
    for the smallest and largest vehicle. The biggest arrow is
    the delivery van, the smallest is the Smart Fortwo.
  - What the arrow view buys you (added): once a row is an arrow,
    you can ask geometric questions about it. How long is it?
    What angle does it make with another arrow? How much of it
    lies along some direction? Every one of those questions has
    a one-line formula, and every one of them is about to matter.

4. NOTATION
  - KEY TERM: scalar: a single number. One measurement, a
    length, an angle, a weight. Written in regular type,
    occasionally a Greek letter (the professor's aside: not
    because he is from Greece, that is just the math convention).
  - KEY TERM: vector: an ordered list of numbers, written in bold
    as v. The numbers are its components.
      Column form: the components stacked vertically. The
        typical way to write it in math.
      Row form: the components side by side, v = [4.6, 1500].
        The computing view. A row of the data table is a vector
        in row form. One record in the dataset.
      Same vector either way. Columns when doing geometry, rows
        when you mean one record.
  - KEY TERM: component: one of the numbers in a vector. For a
    vehicle, the length is one component and the weight is the
    other. (added definition)
  - KEY TERM: theta: the Greek letter used for the angle between
    two arrows. The dot product measures it.
  - KEY TERM: matrix: stack many vectors together, every vehicle
    as a row, and you get a matrix. The whole data table as one
    block of numbers. The entire cloud of points IS a matrix.
  - KEY TERM: dimension: the number of components in a vector,
    which is the number of attributes measured. Two here, so
    everything can be drawn on a flat plot. The math is
    identical at 10 or 100 attributes, you just lose the
    picture. (added definition)
  - For example, three houses measured on two attributes, floor
    area in hundreds of square feet and bedrooms:

      import numpy as np
      m_h = np.array([[12, 2], [18, 3], [25, 4]])
      print(m_h.shape) ## (3, 2): 3 records, 2 attributes
      print(m_h[1]) ## [18 3], one record as a row vector
      print(m_h[:, 0]) ## [12 18 25], one attribute as a column

    The shape (3, 2) is the m by n from the preprocessing intro
    lecture: m rows, one per object, n columns, one per
    attribute.

6. MEASURING FROM THE CENTER OF THE CLOUD
  - For penguins, the origin makes no sense. No penguin is
    anywhere near zero flipper length and zero mass. Arrows from
    (0, 0) would all point the same way toward a far-off corner
    and say nothing about how penguins differ.
  - What you actually want to know is how each penguin differs
    from the average penguin on both measurements. So measure
    from the center of the cloud instead of from the origin.
  - KEY TERM: center of the cloud: the point whose coordinates
    are the mean of each attribute. The average penguin. (added
    definition)
  - KEY TERM: centering: subtracting the mean of each attribute
    from every value, so the cloud's center moves to (0, 0) and
    every arrow starts there. (added definition)
  - Cell 14 of the notebook draws two arrows from the center to
    two penguins at opposite ends of the spectrum. Each arrow's
    direction says which way that penguin differs from average
    and its magnitude says how far off it is.
  - The picture did not change when the data did. Vehicles or
    penguins, it is points in space, arrows for direction and
    magnitude, and a cloud that leans in some direction. That
    lean raises the question the rest of the video answers:
    which direction matters the most?
  - For example, six students measured on hours studied and
    quiz score (score in tens so the axes are comparable, the
    same relabel trick the notebook uses):

      pts = np.array([[2, 5.5], [4, 6.2], [5, 7.0],
                      [7, 7.4], [8, 8.5], [10, 9.0]])
      c = pts.mean(axis=0) ## [6.    7.267], the center
      pc = pts - c ## centered cloud, every arrow from (0, 0)

    The center is 6.0 hours and 7.267 tens of points. The first
    student becomes [-4, -1.767]: four hours under average and
    about 17.7 points under. The last becomes [4, 1.733]. The
    arrows now point in opposite directions, which is the whole
    reason for centering.

7. THE DOT PRODUCT: MEASURING ALIGNMENT
  - Pick any direction, meaning a line through the center. The
    dot product of a penguin's vector with that direction
    measures how far along that direction the penguin reaches.
      Large positive: the vector points the same way as the
        direction.
      Near zero: the vector is perpendicular to the direction.
        Nothing of it lies along that line.
      Negative: the vector points the opposite way. (added)
  - KEY TERM: dot product: for two vectors, multiply the matching
    components and add the products. For v = [v1, v2] and u =
    [u1, u2] that is v1 * u1 + v2 * u2. (added, the arithmetic
    form. The notebook gives the geometric form below.)
  - The geometric form from the notebook: v dot u equals the
    norm of v times the norm of u times the cosine of theta,
    where theta is the angle between them.
  - Reading that formula (added): cosine of 0 degrees is 1, so
    two arrows pointing the same way give the full product of
    their lengths. Cosine of 90 degrees is 0, so perpendicular
    arrows give zero no matter how long they are. Cosine of 180
    degrees is -1. The cosine is the alignment dial, and the two
    norms just scale it by how long the arrows are.
  - Cell 17 of the notebook shows one penguin vector (blue) with
    three green direction lines: aligned, partly aligned,
    perpendicular. The dot product printed in each title
    shrinks as the alignment drops, and in the perpendicular
    panel the projected point lands exactly at the center.
  - For example, v = [3, 4] and w = [4, 1]:

      v, w = np.array([3.0, 4.0]), np.array([4.0, 1.0])
      d = v @ w ## 16.0, same as 3*4 + 4*1
      nv, nw = np.linalg.norm(v), np.linalg.norm(w) ## 5.0, 4.123
      cos_t = d / (nv * nw) ## 0.7761
      ang = np.degrees(np.arccos(cos_t)) ## 39.09 degrees

    Arithmetic form: 3 * 4 + 4 * 1 = 16. Geometric form: 5.0 *
    4.123 * 0.7761 = 16.0. Same number, two routes. The angle
    between the arrows is 39.09 degrees, so they mostly agree on
    direction, and the dot product is a healthy positive number
    because of it.
  - In NumPy the @ operator is the dot product for 1-D arrays
    (and matrix multiplication for 2-D). np.dot does the same.
    (added)

8. NORM, ORTHOGONAL, UNIT, ORTHONORMAL
  - KEY TERM: norm: the length of the arrow, written as v with
    double bars on each side. It is the square root of the dot
    product of the vector with itself.
  - Why that works (added): v dot v is v1 squared plus v2
    squared, and the square root of that is the Pythagorean
    distance from the origin to the tip. The norm is the
    hypotenuse.
  - KEY TERM: orthogonal: two vectors that meet at a right angle.
    Equivalent statement: their dot product is zero. The last
    panel of cell 17 and the right panel of cell 19 show this.
  - KEY TERM: unit vector: a vector of length one.
  - KEY TERM: orthonormal: orthogonal plus unit length. The
    lecture flags this as a term you will need soon.
  - Making a unit vector (added): divide a vector by its own
    norm. The direction is untouched and the length becomes
    exactly 1. Directions are always stored as unit vectors in
    this material, so that a dot product with one reads as a
    pure distance along the line with no length scaling mixed
    in.
  - For example, continuing with v = [3, 4]:

      u = v / nv ## [0.6, 0.8], the unit vector along v
      print(np.linalg.norm(u)) ## 1.0
      a, b = np.array([2.0, 5.0]), np.array([-5.0, 2.0])
      print(a @ b) ## 0.0, orthogonal

    The norm of v is the square root of 3 * 3 + 4 * 4 = 25, so
    5.0, the classic 3-4-5 triangle. Dividing by 5 gives [0.6,
    0.8], whose norm is 1.0. The pair a and b has dot product
    2 * (-5) + 5 * 2 = -10 + 10 = 0, so they are orthogonal.
    Swapping a vector's components and negating one of them is
    the quick way to build a perpendicular in 2-D. Both a and b
    scaled to length 1 would be an orthonormal pair.

9. PROJECTION: DROPPING A VECTOR ONTO A DIRECTION
  - KEY TERM: projection: pick a direction, then drop a
    perpendicular from the tip of the arrow to the line. The
    foot of that perpendicular is the projection. It is how much
    of the penguin lies along that direction.
  - The notebook's statement of the link: the dot product IS the
    projection length, up to the length of the direction. With
    a unit-length direction the dot product is exactly the
    signed distance from the center to the foot of the
    perpendicular. (added: the unit-length qualifier spelled
    out)
  - Two flavors, both used in the notebook code (added):
      Scalar projection: the dot product v dot u, a single
        number, how far along the line.
      Projected point: that number times the unit direction u,
        which gives the coordinates of the foot on the line.
        Cell 21 computes dot = v @ u and proj_pt = dot * u.
  - What is lost (added): the piece of v that does not lie along
    the line is the dashed perpendicular segment in the figure.
    That leftover is itself orthogonal to the direction, which
    is what "foot of the perpendicular" means.
  - For example, v = [3, 4] projected onto the direction 30
    degrees above the x-axis:

      t = np.radians(30)
      u30 = np.array([np.cos(t), np.sin(t)]) ## [0.866, 0.5]
      s = v @ u30 ## 4.5981, scalar projection
      foot = s * u30 ## [3.982, 2.299], the projected point
      left = v - foot ## [-0.982, 1.701], the perpendicular part
      print(left @ u30) ## -2.2e-16, zero up to rounding

    The arrow of length 5 reaches 4.598 along the 30-degree line
    and the rest of it, a piece of length about 1.96, sticks out
    perpendicular. The last print gives -2.2e-16, which is zero
    with floating-point crumbs on it: the leftover really is
    orthogonal to the line. Rotate the direction 90 degrees to
    120 and the same v projects to only 1.964. Same arrow,
    different line, very different amount carried over.

10. PROJECTING THE WHOLE CLOUD: SPREAD DEPENDS ON DIRECTION
  - Now project every penguin onto the line. The 2-D cloud
    collapses to a 1-D spread of points along that direction.
    How spread out those points are depends on which direction
    you chose. Some directions squeeze the penguins together.
    One direction spreads them out the most.
  - KEY TERM: spread: how widely the projected points are
    scattered along the line. The notebook measures it as the
    standard deviation of the projected values. Video 2 replaces
    the word with its formal name, variance, which is that
    standard deviation squared. (added definition)
  - Why the biggest spread is the goal: the more spread the
    projection keeps, the more of the information in the cloud
    survives the squeeze from two numbers per penguin down to
    one. A direction with tiny spread has crushed everyone into
    nearly the same value and thrown the differences away.
  - Cell 23 of the notebook sweeps every direction from 0 to 180
    degrees, keeps the one with the largest spread, and shows it
    flanked by two directions 45 degrees off. The lecture calls
    the panels a so-and-so direction (spread 11.4, labeled "a
    so-so direction" in the notebook), the widest direction
    (spread 15.8), and another direction.
  - This was done by hand, or by brute-force sweep. The next
    video finds the widest direction in an automated way from
    the data itself, using the covariance.
  - For example, the six-student cloud pc from section 6,
    projected onto four directions:

      for ang in (0, 45, 90, 135):
          t = np.radians(ang)
          u = np.array([np.cos(t), np.sin(t)])
          print(ang, (pc @ u).std().round(3)) ## spread

    Output: 0 degrees 2.646, 45 degrees 2.721, 90 degrees 1.216,
    135 degrees 1.036. Zero degrees is the hours axis alone, 90
    is the score axis alone. The 45-degree line beats both of
    them, because the cloud leans up and to the right (more
    hours, higher score) and that diagonal catches both kinds of
    variation at once. At 135 degrees the line runs against the
    lean and the students pile up near the center.
  - Sweeping all 180 whole-degree angles the same way finds the
    best at 24 degrees with spread 2.905. The widest direction is
    not either original axis. It is a tilt of the two, and
    finding that tilt without a brute-force sweep is what PCA
    does. (Run: loop the block above over range(180) and take
    the argmax.)

11. WHERE THIS IS GOING

  - What to carry forward (added): centered data, unit-vector

12. KEY TAKEAWAYS
  - Row of the table = point in space = arrow from the origin.
    Stack the rows and you have a matrix.
  - Scalar is one number, vector is an ordered list of numbers,
    theta is the angle between two vectors.
  - When the origin is meaningless, center the data and measure
    every arrow from the average point.
  - Dot product = norm of v times norm of u times cos theta =
    how far v reaches along u. Zero means perpendicular.
  - Norm = length = square root of v dot v. Orthogonal = dot
    product zero. Unit vector = length one. Orthonormal = both.
  - Projection = foot of the perpendicular on the line. With a
    unit direction, its length is the dot product.
  - Projecting a cloud onto a line gives a 1-D spread that
    depends on the direction. Widest spread keeps the most
    information. Finding it automatically is video 2.
