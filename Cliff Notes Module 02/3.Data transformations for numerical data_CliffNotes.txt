1. WHY TRANSFORM NUMERICAL DATA
  - Most predictive modeling techniques consider many features at
    the same time. The features usually come from different
    domains while sitting in the same dataset.
  - The lecture's example: statistics about people and income.
    Age runs roughly 18 to 70 for working adults. Income runs
    roughly 25,000 to 150,000. Same table, wildly different
    scales.
  - Why that matters in practice: anything that measures
    distance or adds features together (nearest neighbors,
    gradient descent, PCA later in this module) will let the
    feature with the biggest numbers dominate, purely because
    its numbers are bigger. Income would drown age. (added
    explanation)
  - The fix: transform the numerical features into a space that
    allows easy comparison across domains. Two families:
    standardization and scaling.
  - KEY TERM: feature: one numeric column of the dataset, one
    attribute per record. (added definition)
  - KEY TERM: transformation: applying a formula to every value
    of a feature to produce a new version of that feature on a
    different scale. (added definition)
  - All the code in this lesson is provided as Jupyter Notebooks
    for download and experimentation. The three tools are all
    one-liners from scikit-learn's preprocessing module.

2. STANDARDIZATION (Z-SCORE SCALING)
  - KEY TERM: standardization: subtract the feature's mean from
    each value, then divide by the feature's standard deviation.
    Also called variance scaling or Z-score scaling.
  - What it buys you: the transformed feature has a mean of
    exactly zero and a variance of one. Every feature ends up
    on the same footing, "how many standard deviations from
    average is this value," regardless of the original units.
  - Ideal for algorithms that require standardized input or
    perform better with it.
  - Side note from the professor: he has used an adjusted
    Z-score scaling to assign course grades based on performance
    relative to the rest of the class.
  - Two things to watch in the output:
      Negative values appear. Anything below the mean goes
        negative. Make sure whatever algorithm you feed this to
        handles negatives correctly.
      The standard deviation reported by describe() is very
        close to one, not exactly one, on a small dataset. The
        lecture had ten rows and saw the gap shrink when it went
        from five rows to ten. With more items it becomes
        exactly one.
  - Why the gap exists at all: StandardScaler divides by the
    population standard deviation (divide by n). Pandas
    describe() reports the sample standard deviation (divide by
    n minus 1). The ratio between the two is the square root of
    n / (n - 1), which is 1.069 at n = 8 and creeps toward 1 as
    n grows. Same data, two conventions for the same number.
    (added explanation)
  - KEY TERM: population standard deviation: square root of the
    squared differences from the mean summed and divided by n.
    (added definition)
  - KEY TERM: sample standard deviation: the same, but divided
    by n minus 1. (added definition)

3. STANDARDIZATION IN PYTHON
  - The lecture's flow: import pandas, scikit-learn, and NumPy.
    Build a small DataFrame of age and income. Call describe()
    to see mean, standard deviation, min, max, and the 25th,
    50th, and 75th percentiles. Fit and transform with
    StandardScaler. Call describe() again on the result.
  - For example, eight people with height in centimeters and
    salary in dollars:

      import pandas as pd
      from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
      h = [158, 163, 170, 172, 175, 181, 188, 195]
      s = [41000, 52000, 48000, 61000, 75000, 58000, 99000, 250000]
      df_hs = pd.DataFrame({"height_cm": h, "salary": s})
      df_z = pd.DataFrame(StandardScaler().fit_transform(df_hs),
                          columns=df_hs.columns) ## mean 0, var 1
      print(df_z.round(3))
      print(df_z.describe().round(3))

    Before: height has mean 175.25 and salary has mean 85,500.
    Not comparable. After, both columns have mean 0.000 and
    describe() reports std 1.069 for both (the n / (n - 1) gap
    from section 2, with n = 8).
  - Checking one value by hand: height 195 sits 19.75 above the
    mean of 175.25, and the population standard deviation of
    height is 11.573, so 19.75 / 11.573 = 1.707. That matches
    the last row of df_z. The 250,000 salary comes out at 2.553:
    it is 2.5 standard deviations above the mean. The shortest
    person (158) is at -1.491, which is the negative value the
    lecture warns about.
  - Now height 1.707 and salary 2.553 are directly comparable.
    Both say "how unusual is this value for its column."

4. MIN-MAX SCALING
  - KEY TERM: min-max scaling: find the feature's minimum and
    maximum, then convert every value into the 0 to 1 range.
    Subtract the minimum from the value, then divide by the
    distance from minimum to maximum. The lecture's graphic
    shows the original domain compressed onto 0 to 1.
  - What it buys you: the smallest value becomes exactly 0, the
    largest becomes exactly 1, everything else lands in between
    in proportion. No negatives, so it is friendly to algorithms
    that dislike them. The shape of the distribution does not
    change, only its scale. (added explanation)
  - The lecture's Python: bring back the original data, note the
    min and max per column from describe(), then one line with
    MinMaxScaler. The output visibly runs 0 to 1 and describe()
    confirms min 0 and max 1.
  - For example, the same df_hs:

      from sklearn.preprocessing import MinMaxScaler
      df_mm = pd.DataFrame(MinMaxScaler().fit_transform(df_hs),
                           columns=df_hs.columns) ## 0 to 1

    Height 172 becomes (172 - 158) / (195 - 158) = 14 / 37 =
    0.378. Salary 99,000 becomes (99000 - 41000) / (250000 -
    41000) = 0.278. Salary 250,000 becomes 1.000 and 41,000
    becomes 0.000.
  - KEY TERM: outlier: a value that sits far from the rest of its
    column, like the 250,000 salary here. (added definition)
  - The weakness shows in that same salary column: because the
    250,000 outlier owns the value 1.0, every other salary is
    squeezed below 0.28. The median salary lands at 0.089.
    Min-max is only as good as its extremes. (added explanation)

5. ROBUST SCALING
  - KEY TERM: robust scaling: instead of centering on the mean
    (standardization) or the minimum (min-max), center on the
    median. Instead of dividing by the standard deviation or by
    the min-to-max range, divide by the interquartile range.
    The lecture's framing: the transformed data is centered
    using the median instead of the mean, and scaled by the
    interquartile range instead of the range used by min-max.
  - KEY TERM: interquartile range (IQR): 75th percentile minus
    25th percentile. The middle half of the data.
  - What it buys you: the median and the IQR barely move when an
    outlier shows up, while the mean, the standard deviation,
    and especially the min and max jump. So robust scaling is
    ideal for data that contains outliers. The lecture's word:
    this technique is amenable to outliers because it uses the
    median instead of the mean.
  - Again a one-liner (RobustScaler). The output has negative
    values, and once again the big deal is that the two features
    are now in comparable spaces.
  - For example, the same df_hs:

      from sklearn.preprocessing import RobustScaler
      df_rb = pd.DataFrame(RobustScaler().fit_transform(df_hs),
                           columns=df_hs.columns) ## median, IQR

    Height: median 173.5, Q1 168.25, Q3 182.75, IQR 14.5. The
    195 becomes (195 - 173.5) / 14.5 = 1.483 and the 158 becomes
    -1.069. Salary: median 59,500, Q1 51,000, Q3 81,000, IQR
    30,000. The 250,000 becomes (250000 - 59500) / 30000 = 6.35.
  - Read that 6.35 next to the same value's other two scores
    (2.553 standardized, 1.000 min-max). Robust scaling pushes
    the outlier furthest from the bulk of the column, and by
    construction the middle half of the column lands inside a
    band one unit wide that straddles zero. describe() on df_rb
    shows the 50th percentile at exactly 0.000 for both columns,
    which is the median centering doing its job. (added
    explanation)

6. PICKING ONE
  - Standardization: the lecture's pick for algorithms that
    require or perform better with standardized input. Sensitive
    to outliers through the mean and standard deviation. (added
    explanation)
  - Min-max: when you need a hard 0 to 1 range and no negatives.
    Very sensitive to outliers because the extremes define the
    range. (added explanation)
  - Robust: when the data has outliers you do not want to remove.
  - All three are fit on the data (they learn the mean and std,
    the min and max, or the median and IQR from it) and then
    applied. The word "fit" in fit_transform is the learning
    step and "transform" is the application. (added explanation)
  - None of them change the order of values within a column or
    the relative spacing between them. They change the ruler,
    not the data. (added explanation)

7. KEY TAKEAWAYS
  - Features from different domains need a common scale before
    a model sees them together.
  - Standardization: (x - mean) / standard deviation. Result:
    mean 0, variance 1. Also called Z-score or variance scaling.
    Produces negatives.
  - Min-max: (x - min) / (max - min). Result: 0 to 1.
  - Robust: center on the median, divide by IQR. Best with
    outliers.
  - All three are one-liners in scikit-learn: StandardScaler,
    MinMaxScaler, RobustScaler.

8. QUIZ-SAFE FACTS
  - Reason for transforming: modeling techniques consider many
    features at once and those features come from different
    domains.
  - Standardization: subtract the mean, divide by the standard
    deviation. Transformed mean is zero, variance is one.
  - Other names for standardization: variance scaling, Z-score
    scaling.
  - Standardization is ideal for algorithms that require or
    perform better with standardized input.
  - The lecture files min-max and robust scaling under data
    scaling, as two options separate from standardization.
  - Standardized output contains negative values.
  - Standard deviation after standardization on a small dataset
    is very close to one, not exactly, and gets closer as the
    number of items grows.
  - Min-max scaling converts values to the 0 to 1 range using
    the feature's minimum and maximum.
  - Robust scaling centers using the median and scales by the
    interquartile range (75th minus 25th percentile). Ideal for
    data with outliers.
  - The lecture's example data: ten rows of age (22 to 70) and
    income (35,000 to 140,000), age mean 47, income mean 74,500.
  - describe() reports mean, standard deviation, min, max, and
    the 25th, 50th, 75th percentiles.
