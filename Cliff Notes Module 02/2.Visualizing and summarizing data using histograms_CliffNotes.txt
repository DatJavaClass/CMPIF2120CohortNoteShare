1. CENTRAL TENDENCY: MEAN, WEIGHTED MEAN
  - The lesson is about summarizing numerical data with as few
    numbers as possible, then drawing it as a histogram.
  - KEY TERM: central tendency: a single number that stands for
    the center of a set of values. The mean, median, and mode are
    the three measures covered. (added definition)
  - KEY TERM: mean (average): with N data points x sub i, add
    them up and divide by N. Surprisingly common and very
    effective as a measure of the center.
  - KEY TERM: weighted arithmetic mean (weighted average): when
    each value carries a weight, multiply each value by its
    weight, add those products up, and divide by the SUM OF THE
    WEIGHTS, not by N. The assumption is that all weights are
    positive, so the sum of weights can never be zero and you
    never divide by zero. The class will definitely use this.
  - For example, three quiz scores of 88, 72, and 95 with weights
    0.2, 0.3, and 0.5. The plain mean is (88 + 72 + 95) / 3 =
    85.0. The weighted mean is (88 * 0.2 + 72 * 0.3 + 95 * 0.5)
    / (0.2 + 0.3 + 0.5) = 86.7 / 1.0 = 86.7. The heavy weight on
    the 95 pulls the answer up. Dividing by the weight sum is
    what keeps the result on the same scale as the scores. With
    weights 0.4, 0.6, and 1.0 (sum 2) the weighted sum is 173.4.
    Dividing by N gives 57.8. Dividing by the weight sum gives
    the correct 86.7. (added explanation)

2. MEDIAN AND MODE
  - KEY TERM: median: sort the values from lowest to highest and
    take the one exactly on the border between the lower half and
    the upper half. With an odd count that is the middle value.
    With an even count there is no single middle value, so the
    median is defined as the mean of the two middle values.
  - For example, the values 15, 2, 9, 4, 11 sort to 2, 4, 9, 11,
    15 and the median is 9. Drop the 11 and you have 2, 4, 9, 15,
    an even count, so the median is (4 + 9) / 2 = 6.5.
  - KEY TERM: mode: the value that appears most often, the one
    with the highest frequency.
      KEY TERM: unimodal: one value has the top frequency.
      KEY TERM: bimodal: two values tie for the top frequency.
      Three or more tied at the top: trimodal and so on (added
        term, the lecture names only unimodal and bimodal). The
        lecture's wording for three: three numbers that all have
        the exact same frequency, and that frequency is the
        highest over everybody else.
      If every value shows up only once, there is no mode. There
        is no point in calling every number the mode.
  - For example, 2, 5, 5, 8, 9, 9, 9 is unimodal with mode 9. The
    set 1, 4, 4, 6, 6 is bimodal with modes 4 and 6. The set 3,
    8, 1 has no mode.
  - KEY TERM: frequency: how many times a value occurs in the
    data. The y-axis of every histogram in this lesson. (added
    definition)

3. SYMMETRIC VERSUS SKEWED DATA
  - In the ideal symmetric case the mean, median, and mode are
    all the same value (see the lecture's symmetric distribution
    plot). This almost never happens in real life.
  - KEY TERM: skew: the direction in which a distribution's tail
    stretches, which pulls the mean away from the median and
    mode. (added definition)
      KEY TERM: positively skewed: mean > median > mode.
      KEY TERM: negatively skewed: mean < median < mode.
  - The salary example. Plot employee salaries with frequency on
    the y-axis. Once the CEO is included, the mean lands to the
    right of the median, because that is how the math works out:
    one huge value drags the sum, and therefore the mean, upward,
    while the median only cares about which value sits in the
    middle of the sorted list. (added explanation)
  - For example, eight salaries in thousands: 42, 45, 48, 50, 52,
    55, 60, 400. The mean is 94.0. The median is 51.0. Nobody but
    the 400 earns anywhere near 94, so "the average salary here
    is 94" is true and useless.
  - Takeaway: the average is good, but it should never be the
    only number you report. Outliers like the CEO artificially
    inflate it.

4. DISPERSION: WHY THE MEAN IS NOT ENOUGH
  - KEY TERM: dispersion: how spread out the data is around its
    center.
  - The lecture's demonstration uses three datasets with exactly
    the same average but very different shapes: one spread
    uniformly across its range, one pinned to a single value, and
    one bimodal with half the values at each extreme.
  - For example, ten test scores in each of three classes, all
    three with mean 80.0:
      Class 1: 60, 70, 80, 90, 100 twice over. Uniform across the
        range. Variance 200.0, standard deviation 14.14.
      Class 2: ten scores of 80. Pinpoint. Variance 0, standard
        deviation 0. The mean describes every student perfectly.
      Class 3: 60 and 100 alternating. Bimodal. Variance 400.0,
        standard deviation 20.0. Nobody scored 80. The mean
        describes no student at all.
  - The lecture's framing for the bimodal case: movie ratings from
    1 to 5 where half the audience hates the film and half loves
    it. Calling it "an average movie" does not do it justice. The
    fix is to plot the distribution and see the shape.
  - KEY TERM: uniform distribution: every value in the range
    occurs with about the same frequency. (added definition)
  - KEY TERM: bimodal distribution: two separate peaks of
    frequency. In the lecture's example, equal numbers of ones
    and fives.
  - KEY TERM: range: the distance from the minimum value to the
    maximum value. (added definition)

5. QUARTILES, PERCENTILES, BOXPLOTS, IQR
  - KEY TERM: quartiles: split the sorted data at five points of
    importance, which makes four chunks. Q1 is the bottom 25th
    percentile, meaning the bottom quarter of the data falls
    below it. Q2 is the median. Q3 is the 75th percentile.
  - The lecture's recipe for finding them by hand: find the
    median (Q2). Then take the lower half, from the minimum up to
    the median, and find its median. That is Q1. Do the same for
    the upper half, from the median to the maximum. That is Q3.
  - KEY TERM: percentile: the same idea with 100 chunks instead
    of four. The p-th percentile is the value below which p
    percent of the data falls. (added definition)
  - KEY TERM: boxplot: a drawing of the five-number summary.
    Bottom line is the minimum, top line is the maximum, the
    box runs from Q1 (bottom edge) to Q3 (top edge), and the line
    inside the box is the median. Good for comparing many sources
    or many points in time side by side.
  - KEY TERM: five-number summary: minimum, Q1, median, Q3,
    maximum.
  - KEY TERM: interquartile range (IQR): the height of the box,
    Q3 minus Q1.
  - Outlier rule: the definition normally used is that a value
    more than 1.5 times the IQR beyond the box is an outlier
    (above Q3 by more than 1.5 IQR, or below Q1 by more than 1.5
    IQR). The lecture's boxplot slide marks the identified
    outliers in the yellow area.
  - For example, ten sorted values: 3, 5, 7, 8, 12, 13, 14, 18,
    21, 40. Even count, so the median is (12 + 13) / 2 = 12.5.
    Lower half 3, 5, 7, 8, 12 has median 7, so Q1 = 7. Upper half
    13, 14, 18, 21, 40 has median 18, so Q3 = 18. IQR = 18 - 7 =
    11. The fences (outlier cutoffs) sit at 7 - 1.5 * 11 = -9.5
    and 18 + 1.5 * 11 = 34.5. The 40 is past the upper fence, so
    it is the outlier. Minimum 3, maximum 40, range 37.

6. VARIANCE AND STANDARD DEVIATION
  - A second way to measure dispersion, next to plotting it.
  - KEY TERM: variance: for each value x sub i, take its
    difference from the average, square that difference, add all
    the squares up, and divide by n.
  - KEY TERM: standard deviation: the square root of the
    variance. It is back in the original units, which is why it
    is the number people quote. (last sentence added)
  - Why square? Differences above and below the mean would cancel
    if you just added them (the sum of raw differences from the
    mean is always zero). Squaring makes every difference
    positive and makes big misses count for more than small
    ones. (added explanation)
  - The lecture's verdict: the average plus this one extra number
    is a good statistic to have about any numerical data.
  - For example, the values 6, 8, 10, 12, 14. Mean 10. Differences
    from the mean: -4, -2, 0, 2, 4. Squared: 16, 4, 0, 4, 16. Sum
    40, divided by n = 5 gives variance 8.0. Square root gives
    standard deviation 2.83.

7. HISTOGRAMS
  - KEY TERM: histogram: a plot representing the frequencies of
    the data as rectangles. The trick is that you do not always
    give every value its own rectangle. You combine values into
    buckets.
  - KEY TERM: bucket (bin): a range of values that share one
    rectangle in a histogram. (added definition)
  - Two main types:
      KEY TERM: equal width histogram: each bucket covers the
        same portion of the x-axis. The range of each bucket is
        fixed.
      KEY TERM: equal frequency (equal height) histogram: the
        buckets are built so that each one's frequency, its
        height on the y-axis, is about constant. "About" because
        fractions are not allowed, as the construction shows.
  - The trivial histogram gives every distinct value its own
    bucket. That looks reasonable for a small dataset but on any
    real data you cannot see what is going on, which is why you
    combine values into range buckets.
  - The lecture works its examples on a table of store prices
    with a count of how often each price appears. The examples
    below use a different price table so you can check the
    method rather than memorize the answer.

8. BUILDING AN EQUAL WIDTH HISTOGRAM
  - The recipe:
      Range = max minus min plus 1.
      Width = range divided by the number of buckets you were
        told to use.
      First bucket starts at the minimum and covers one width.
        Each next bucket starts where the last one ended.
      Add up the frequencies of the values that fall in each
        bucket.
  - For example, prices and counts:

      price:  1  4  7  9 11 12 15 17 19 22 24
      count:  3  6  2  5  7  4  9  3  5  2  4

    Total count is 50. Suppose four buckets. Range is 24 - 1 + 1
    = 24. Width is 24 / 4 = 6. Buckets are 1 to 6, 7 to 12, 13
    to 18, and 19 to 24.
      1 to 6 holds prices 1 and 4: frequency 3 + 6 = 9.
      7 to 12 holds 7, 9, 11, 12: frequency 2 + 5 + 7 + 4 = 18.
      13 to 18 holds 15 and 17: frequency 9 + 3 = 12.
      19 to 24 holds 19, 22, 24: frequency 5 + 2 + 4 = 11.
    Check: 9 + 18 + 12 + 11 = 50. The heights vary, the widths
    do not. That is the whole definition.

9. BUILDING AN EQUAL FREQUENCY HISTOGRAM
  - The recipe:
      Add up all the counts to get the total frequency.
      Target height = total divided by the number of buckets.
      Walk the values in order, adding counts into the current
        bucket. This is a little bit like counting cards in
        poker.
      When adding the next value would cross the target, compare
        the running total just below the target with the running
        total just above it. Whichever is CLOSER to the target
        wins. It is not poker: going over does not lose. The
        value that was left out starts the next bucket. (added:
        a running total that lands exactly on the target is the
        closest possible, so stop there.)
      Do the math for the last bucket too, even though it takes
        whatever is left. If the last bucket does not check out,
        something went wrong earlier. (The professor's advice
        for multiple choice is the same: look at all the answers
        before deciding.)
  - For example, the same price table with three buckets. Total
    is 50, target is 50 / 3 = 16.67.
      Bucket 1: 3 (price 1), then 9 (price 4), then 11 (price
        7), then 16 (price 9). Adding 11's count of 7 would make
        23.
        Compare 16 and 23 against 16.67: 16 is closer. Stop.
        Bucket 1 is prices 1 through 9, frequency 16.
      Bucket 2: 7 (price 11), then 11 (price 12), and adding
        15's count of 9 makes 20. Compare 11 and 20
        against 16.67: 20 is 3.33 away, 11 is 5.67 away, so 20
        wins and 15 is included. Bucket 2 is prices 11 through
        15, frequency 20.
      Bucket 3: 3 (price 17), 8 (price 19), 10 (price 22), 14
        (price 24). Never reaches the target, and nothing is left
        over.
        Bucket 3 is prices 17 through 24, frequency 14.
    Check: 16 + 20 + 14 = 50. Heights are 16, 20, 14, all near
    the target of 16.67. Widths on the x-axis are 1 to 9, 11 to
    15, 17 to 24, nowhere near equal. That is the trade the
    equal frequency histogram makes.

10. KEY TAKEAWAYS
  - Mean is the workhorse but is dragged by outliers. Median is
    the middle of the sorted list. Mode is the most frequent
    value.
  - Weighted mean divides by the sum of the weights.
  - Positive skew: mean > median > mode. Negative skew: the
    reverse. Symmetric: all three equal, almost never in real
    data.
  - Same mean, different shapes: always look at the distribution.
  - Five-number summary drives the boxplot. IQR = Q3 - Q1.
    Outliers sit more than 1.5 IQR outside the box.
  - Variance = mean of squared differences from the mean.
    Standard deviation = its square root.
  - Equal width: fix the x-axis chunk. Equal frequency: fix the
    y-axis height and pick the running total closest to the
    target at each break.

11. QUIZ-SAFE FACTS
  - Weighted arithmetic mean: sum of (value times weight) divided
    by the sum of the weights. Weights assumed positive.
  - Median of an even-count set: mean of the two middle values.
  - Unimodal, bimodal, or three or more tied at the top. All
    values appearing once: no mode.
  - Positively skewed: mean > median > mode. Negatively skewed:
    mean < median < mode.
  - Quartiles: five points of importance, four chunks. Q1 = 25th
    percentile, Q2 = median, Q3 = 75th percentile. Q1 is the
    median of the lower half, Q3 the median of the upper half.
  - Boxplot five-number summary: minimum, Q1, median, Q3,
    maximum. Box from Q1 to Q3, line inside at the median.
  - IQR = Q3 - Q1. Outlier: more than 1.5 times the IQR outside
    the box.
  - Variance: squared differences from the average, summed,
    divided by n. Standard deviation: square root of variance.
  - Histogram types: equal width and equal frequency (equal
    height).
  - Equal width range: max - min + 1. Width: range / number of
    buckets.
  - Equal frequency target: total count / number of buckets. At
    each break pick the running total closest to the target.
