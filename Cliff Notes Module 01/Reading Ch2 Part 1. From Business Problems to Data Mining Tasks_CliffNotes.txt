1. CHAPTER FRAMING
================================================================
  - Fundamental concepts: canonical data mining tasks; the data
    mining process; supervised vs unsupervised mining.
  - Core principle: data mining is a PROCESS with well-understood
    stages. Some are IT work (automated pattern discovery and
    evaluation); others need an analyst's creativity, business
    knowledge, and common sense. Knowing the process makes
    projects systematic instead of "heroic endeavors driven by
    chance and individual acumen." It is also the book's
    overarching framework.
  - Chapter order: task types (this note), then the process
    (CRISP-DM, next note), then adjacent topics the book skips
    (databases, data warehousing, basic statistics).

  KEY TERMS
  - Data mining: automated extraction of patterns and models from
    data; treated here as a staged process, not one algorithm
    run. (added definition)
  - Individual: an entity we have data about. Usually a customer
    or consumer, but can be inanimate, e.g. a business. Made
    precise in Chapter 3.

================================================================
2. DECOMPOSING BUSINESS PROBLEMS INTO TASKS
================================================================
  - Every data-driven business problem is unique: its own goals,
    desires, constraints, even personalities. As in engineering,
    common tasks underlie them.
  - Data scientists, with business stakeholders, DECOMPOSE the
    problem into subtasks, solve them, then RECOMPOSE the
    solutions. Some subtasks are unique to the business; others
    are common data mining tasks with known solutions.
  - MegaTelCo churn example: specifics are unique, but one
    subtask appears in any churn problem: estimate from
    historical data the probability that a customer terminates
    her contract shortly after it expires. Once data are in the
    right format (Chapter 3), that estimation is a standard task.
  - TIP (book sidebar): a critical skill is breaking an analytics
    problem into pieces that each match a known task with
    existing tools. Avoids reinventing the wheel; focuses human
    creativity on what is not automated.
  - Despite countless algorithms, only a handful of fundamentally
    different task TYPES exist. Most basic form of the first two:
    finding "correlations" between one variable describing an
    individual (e.g. left after contract expired) and others.

  KEY TERMS
  - Churn: customers leaving a company (here, a telecom) for a
    competitor or cancelling service; churn prediction estimates
    who is likely to leave. (added definition)
  - Correlation: a statistical association between two variables,
    such that knowing one tells you something about the other.
    (added definition)

================================================================
3. THE NINE COMMON DATA MINING TASKS
================================================================

  3.1 CLASSIFICATION and CLASS PROBABILITY ESTIMATION
  - Predict, for each individual, which of a small set of
    (usually mutually exclusive) classes it belongs to. Example:
    "Among all the customers of MegaTelCo, which are likely to
    respond to a given offer?" Classes: will respond / will not
    respond. A classification model assigns each new individual a class.
  - Closely related: SCORING (class probability estimation). A
    scoring model outputs, per class, a probability or other
    likelihood score instead of a hard label. Each customer gets
    a score for how likely they are to respond.
  - A model that does one can usually be modified to do the other.

  3.2 REGRESSION ("value estimation")
  - Estimate or predict, for each individual, the numerical value
    of some variable.
  - Example: "How much will a given customer use the service?"
    Target is usage; model built from similar individuals'
    historical usage.
  - Informal contrast: classification predicts WHETHER something
    will happen; regression predicts HOW MUCH.

  3.3 SIMILARITY MATCHING
  - Identify individuals similar to a given one, from known data.
    Example: IBM finds companies similar to its best business
    customers, using "firmographic" data, to point its sales
    force at the best opportunities.
  - Basis of one of the most popular recommendation methods (find
    people similar to you in what they liked or bought). Also
    underlies solutions to classification, regression, and
    clustering. Chapter 6.

  3.4 CLUSTERING
  - Group individuals by similarity, NOT driven by any specific
    purpose. Example: "Do our customers form natural groups or
    segments?"
  - Uses: preliminary domain exploration (natural groups may
    suggest further tasks); input to decisions such as what
    products to offer or develop, or how to structure customer
    care and sales teams. Chapter 6.

  3.5 CO-OCCURRENCE GROUPING
  - Also called frequent itemset mining, association rule
    discovery, and market-basket analysis.
  - Find associations between entities based on the transactions
    they appear in together. Example: "What items are commonly
    purchased together?" Supermarket records may show ground meat
    bought with hot sauce far more often than expected; acting on
    it (promotion, display, combo offer) takes creativity.
  - Versus clustering: clustering uses similarity of the objects'
    ATTRIBUTES; co-occurrence uses similarity based on APPEARING
    TOGETHER in transactions.
  - Some recommendation systems do a form of affinity grouping
    ("people who bought X also bought Y").
  - Output: descriptions of items that occur together, usually
    with co-occurrence frequency statistics and an estimate of
    how surprising the co-occurrence is.

  3.6 PROFILING (behavior description)
  - Characterize the typical behavior of an individual, group, or
    population. Scales from a whole population down to small
    groups or single individuals.
  - Example: "What is the typical cell phone usage of this
    customer segment?" May need a complex description: night and
    weekend airtime averages, international usage, roaming
    charges, text minutes, and so on.
  - Often sets behavioral norms for ANOMALY DETECTION: fraud
    detection, computer intrusion monitoring (someone breaking
    into your iTunes account). Given a person's usual credit card
    purchases, a new charge is checked against the profile; the
    degree of mismatch becomes a suspicion score and an alarm
    fires if it is too high.

  3.7 LINK PREDICTION
  - Predict connections between data items, usually by suggesting
    a link should exist, possibly also estimating link strength.
    Social network example: "Since you and Karen share 10
    friends, maybe you'd like to be Karen's friend?"
  - Movie recommendation: build a graph between customers and the
    movies they watched or rated, then search for links that do
    NOT exist yet but are predicted to exist and be strong. Those
    become the recommendations.

  3.8 DATA REDUCTION
  - Replace a large dataset with a smaller one that keeps much of
    the important information. The smaller set may be easier to
    process and may reveal the information better.
  - Example: a massive movie-viewing dataset reduced to a small
    dataset of latent taste preferences (e.g. genre preferences).
  - Usually loses information; what matters is the trade-off for
    improved insight.

  3.9 CAUSAL MODELING
  - Understand which events or actions actually INFLUENCE others.
    Example: a predictive model targets ads, and targeted
    consumers buy at a higher rate. Did the ads cause purchases,
    or did the model pick people who would have bought anyway?
  - Techniques: those needing heavy data investment, such as
    randomized controlled experiments ("A/B tests"), and
    sophisticated methods for drawing causal conclusions from
    observational data. Both are "counterfactual" analysis:
    compare two situations that cannot both happen, the
    "treatment" (e.g. showing an ad) happening vs not happening.
  - A careful data scientist ALWAYS states the exact assumptions
    needed for a causal conclusion to hold; there always are
    some. Business trade-off: invest more to reduce the
    assumptions, or accept the conclusions as good enough given
    them.
  - Even careful randomized experiments carry assumptions that
    can invalidate conclusions. The "placebo effect" in medicine
    is the notorious case of an overlooked assumption.

  Book scope: principles underlying all nine, illustrated mainly
  with classification, regression, similarity matching, and
  clustering.

  KEY TERMS
  - Class: one of a small set of categories an individual may
    belong to; the categorical target of classification.
  - Score: a number representing the probability or likelihood
    that an individual belongs to a class.
  - Firmographic data: attributes describing companies (industry,
    size, location, revenue); the business analogue of
    demographics. (added definition)
  - Recommendation system: software that suggests items to a user
    based on similarity, co-occurrence, or predicted links.
    (added definition)
  - Market-basket analysis: co-occurrence grouping applied to
    purchase transactions.
  - Association rule: a discovered pattern of the form "if X is
    in the transaction, Y tends to be too," with frequency and
    surprise statistics. (added definition)
  - Anomaly detection: flagging items whose behavior deviates
    sharply from an established profile or norm. (added
    definition)
  - Randomized controlled experiment / A/B test: randomly assign
    individuals to a treatment or a control group so outcome
    differences can be attributed to the treatment. (added
    definition)
  - Observational data: data collected without the analyst
    controlling who received the treatment. (added definition)
  - Counterfactual: the outcome that would have occurred under
    the alternative that did not happen. (added definition)
  - Latent: not directly observed, but inferable from the data
    (e.g. genre preference from viewing). (added definition)

4. SUPERVISED VERSUS UNSUPERVISED METHODS
================================================================
  - Which task fits churn? Practitioners often frame churn as
    finding SEGMENTS of customers more or less likely to leave.
    That sounds like classification, or clustering, or even
    regression. Choosing needs the distinctions below.
  - Two similar-sounding questions about a customer base:
      "Do our customers naturally fall into different groups?"
        No specific purpose or TARGET for the grouping:
        UNSUPERVISED.
      "Can we find groups of customers who have particularly
      high likelihoods of canceling their service soon after
      their contracts expire?"
        A specific target is defined (will she leave when the
        contract expires?) and the segmentation exists to act on
        churn likelihood: SUPERVISED.
  - Origin of the terms (book sidebar): machine learning. A
    teacher "supervises" the learner by supplying targets with
    the examples. Unsupervised learning uses the same examples
    with no targets; the learner must form its own conclusions
    about what the examples have in common.
  - Why it matters: if a specific target CAN be provided, phrase
    the problem as supervised. Supervised tasks need different
    techniques and their results are often much more useful,
    because the grouping is aimed at predicting the target.
    Clustering (unsupervised) groups by similarity with no
    guarantee the groups are meaningful or useful for any purpose.
  - Second condition for supervised mining: there must be DATA on
    the target, not just a target definable in principle.
    Example: knowing whether a customer stays six months is
    useful, but if data are retained only two months, target
    values cannot be produced. Acquiring target data is often a
    key data science investment.
  - The target value for an individual is its LABEL; the word
    reflects that one often has to spend money to label data.

  Task-to-method mapping (per the book)
  - Generally supervised: classification, regression, causal
    modeling.
  - Could be either: similarity matching, link prediction, data
    reduction.
  - Generally unsupervised: clustering, co-occurrence grouping,
    profiling.

  Classification vs regression: decided by TARGET TYPE
  - Regression: numeric target. Classification: categorical
    (often binary) target.
  - Three worked questions:
      "Will this customer purchase service S1 if given incentive
      I?" Classification, binary target (purchases or not).
      "Which service package (S1, S2, or none) will a customer
      likely purchase if given incentive I?" Classification,
      three-valued target.
      "How much will this customer use the service?" Regression,
      numeric target (usage per customer, actual or predicted).
  - Subtlety: business often wants a NUMERICAL prediction over a
    CATEGORICAL target. In churn, yes/no may not be enough; we
    want the probability the customer continues. Still
    classification (the target is categorical), not regression.
    Where clarity matters: "class probability estimation."
  - Two vital early decisions in any project:
      (i) supervised or unsupervised line of attack;
      (ii) if supervised, a PRECISE definition of the target
      variable: a specific quantity that is the focus of the
      mining and for which values can be obtained for some
      example data. Revisited in Chapter 3.

  KEY TERMS
  - Segment: a subgroup of customers sharing some characteristic
    or behavior. (added definition)
  - Supervised learning: the data include a target value for each
    example, and the model learns to predict that target.
  - Unsupervised learning: no target is given; the method finds
    structure (groups, associations, profiles) on its own.
  - Target (target variable): the specific quantity the model is
    built to predict.
  - Label: the target value recorded for a single individual.
  - Categorical variable: takes one of a fixed set of discrete
    values (e.g. accepted / rejected). (added definition)
  - Binary target: a categorical target with exactly two values.
    (added definition)
  - Numeric variable: takes a measurable number (e.g. minutes of
    usage). (added definition)
  - Class probability estimation: classification whose output is
    the probability of each class rather than a single class.

5. DATA MINING VERSUS THE USE OF ITS RESULTS
================================================================
  - Second key distinction: (1) MINING the data to find patterns
    and build models, vs (2) USING the results of the mining.
  - Students and managers alike confuse them. Intended use should
    inform the mining, but the two stay distinct.
  - Churn deployment scenario: mining creates a class probability
    estimation model M. For each existing customer, described by
    a set of characteristics, M takes those as input and produces
    a score or probability of attrition. That is the USE. The
    mining produced M from other, often historical, data.

  Figure 2-1, "Data mining versus the use of data mining results"
  - Top half (Mining): a Historical Data table with columns x, y,
    z, class. Sample rows: 14 / True / Red / accepted; 6 / True /
    Blue / rejected; 50.3 / False / Red / accepted. Caption:
    "Training data have all values specified." An arrow through a
    Data mining step (magnifier icon) produces a Model (drawn as
    a decision tree). "Model is deployed."
  - Bottom half (Use): a New data item, 30 / false / Red / ?,
    class unknown ("e.g. will customer accept?"). Fed to the
    deployed model, output: "Class: accepted, Probability: 0.88."
  - Point: historical data have the target ("class") filled in;
    the model then predicts both the class value and its
    probability for new data whose class is unknown.

  KEY TERMS
  - Model: the pattern or rule set produced by data mining that
    maps an individual's characteristics to a prediction. (added
    definition)
  - Training data: the historical examples, with targets
    specified, used to build the model. (added definition)
  - Deployment: putting the finished model into operation on new
    cases. (added definition)
  - Attrition: customer loss; used here as a synonym for churn.
    (added definition)
  - Decision tree: a model that predicts by walking a series of
    attribute tests from a root node down to a leaf holding the
    prediction; the tree in Figure 2-1 is one. (added definition)

================================================================
6. KEY TAKEAWAYS
================================================================
  - Decompose the business problem into common data mining
    subtasks; recompose the solutions.
  - Nine task types: classification, regression, similarity
    matching, clustering, co-occurrence grouping, profiling, link
    prediction, data reduction, causal modeling.
  - Classification = whether; regression = how much. Both are
    "find correlations with a target" at their most basic.
  - Supervised = a target is defined AND target data exist.
    Unsupervised = no target; results may not be useful for any
    given purpose.
  - Probability of a categorical outcome is still classification
    (class probability estimation), not regression.
  - Mining (building the model) and use (applying it) are
    different activities; use should inform mining.
  - Causal claims always rest on assumptions; state them.

7. QUIZ-SAFE FACTS
================================================================
  - Classification target: categorical (often binary). Regression
    target: numeric.
  - Generally supervised: classification, regression, causal
    modeling. Generally unsupervised: clustering, co-occurrence
    grouping, profiling. Either: similarity matching, link
    prediction, data reduction.
  - Co-occurrence grouping aliases: frequent itemset mining,
    association rule discovery, market-basket analysis.
  - Profiling alias: behavior description. Regression alias:
    value estimation.
  - Clustering groups by attribute similarity; co-occurrence
    grouping groups by appearing together in transactions.
  - The target value for one individual is its label.
  - Supervised mining needs data on the target, not just a target
    definable in principle.
  - A/B tests are randomized controlled experiments; causal
    modeling methods are counterfactual analysis.
  - Placebo effect: the book's example of an overlooked
    assumption in randomized experimentation.
  - Figure 2-1 output for the new item (30, false, Red): Class
    accepted, Probability 0.88.
  - IBM uses "firmographic" data for similarity matching to find
    companies like its best customers.
  - Link prediction example: "you and Karen share 10 friends."
  - Supermarket co-occurrence example: ground meat with hot sauce.
