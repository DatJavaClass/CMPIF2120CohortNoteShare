1. WHY A PROCESS AT ALL
================================================================
  - Book's framing: data mining is a craft. Heavy on science and
    technology, but application still involves art. Like other
    mature crafts it has a well-understood process that structures
    the problem and buys consistency, repeatability, objectivity.
  - KEY TERM: data mining. Extracting useful knowledge, patterns,
    or regularities from data, typically via automated techniques,
    to support business decisions. (added definition)
  - KEY TERM: CRISP-DM. Cross Industry Standard Process for Data
    Mining (book cites Shearer, 2000). A six-stage, iterative
    workflow for a data mining / data science project. Victor's
    notes: the point is to not jump straight into building a model
    before you understand the problem.
  - History from Victor's notes only: dates from 1999; tool- and
    industry-agnostic by design, which is why it survived into an
    era it was never built for. (Book gives only Shearer 2000.)
  - Setup from p. 8, Figure 2-1: keep two things straight, (1)
    mining data to find patterns and build models versus (2) using
    the results. Students and managers confuse them. Churn: mining
    produces a class probability estimation model M from historical
    data; use means feeding a current customer's characteristics
    into M and getting an attrition probability out. Figure 2-1
    draws mining on top, use on the bottom.
      - KEY TERM: class probability estimation. Supervised task
        outputting, per individual, an estimated probability of
        belonging to each class (here: churn / not). (added
        definition)
      - KEY TERM: churn / attrition. Customers leaving for a
        competitor or ending service. (added definition)

2. FIGURE 2-2: THE CRISP CYCLE
================================================================
  - Layout: clockwise outer loop, six boxes inside, a "Data"
    cylinder at the center.
  - Arrows inside the loop:
      - Business Understanding <-> Data Understanding (two-way)
      - Data Understanding -> Data Preparation
      - Data Preparation <-> Modeling (two-way)
      - Modeling -> Evaluation
      - Evaluation -> Deployment
      - Evaluation -> Business Understanding (the "shortcut")
      - Outer ring: Deployment back to Business Understanding.
  - Book's reading: iteration is the rule. One pass without solving
    the problem is generally not a failure; the first pass is often
    data exploration, the second is far better informed.
  - Victor's notes, same point: a cycle, not a line. The outer loop
    matters because deployment feeds new business questions.
    Evaluation can reveal a need for different data, modeling can
    expose data-prep problems, exploration can reveal a badly
    formulated business question. Move backward and forward as
    needed.
  - Victor's notes name the two most common loops as 1<->2 and
    3<->4. Read under the standard 1-6 numbering (his "phases 1 and
    6" remark implies it) that is Business <-> Data Understanding
    and Data Preparation <-> Modeling: exactly the two two-way
    arrows in Figure 2-2.

================================================================
3. THE SIX STAGES
================================================================
Numbering below is the standard 1-6. (Victor's file leaves Business
Understanding unnumbered and numbers the rest 1-5; same six stages,
same order.)

  3.1 BUSINESS UNDERSTANDING
  - Understand the problem to be solved. Business projects seldom
    arrive as clean data mining problems; recasting the problem and
    designing a solution is itself iterative discovery ("cycles
    within a cycle"). The first formulation is rarely complete or
    optimal.
  - Where analyst creativity matters most. Book's tip: the key to a
    great success is often a creative problem formulation, i.e. how
    to cast the business problem as one or more data science
    problems. Knowing the fundamentals helps you see novel
    formulations.
  - Practical move: engineer the problem so subproblems map onto
    the standard tasks (classification, regression, probability
    estimation, etc.) from the earlier section "From Business
    Problems to Data Mining Tasks."
      - KEY TERM: classification. Supervised task predicting which
        of a small set of classes an individual belongs to. (added
        definition)
      - KEY TERM: regression. Supervised task predicting a numeric
        value for an individual. (added definition)
  - Think about the problem AND the use scenario: what exactly do
    we want to do, how, and which parts could be data mining
    models. Book calls this one of the most important fundamental
    principles of data science (Ch. 7 and 11 are devoted to it).
    Expect to loop back and adjust the use scenario to fit the real
    business need.
  - Framing the business problem in terms of expected value lets
    you decompose it systematically into data mining tasks (detail
    in later chapters).
      - KEY TERM: expected value. Probability-weighted average of a
        decision's outcomes: sum over outcomes of
        p(outcome) * value(outcome). (added definition)
  - Victor's example: "We want to predict which customers are
    likely to cancel their subscriptions so we can intervene."
    Same churn framing the book uses.

  3.2 DATA UNDERSTANDING
  - Data are the raw material. Know their strengths and limits;
    data rarely match the problem exactly. Historical data were
    usually collected for unrelated purposes or none at all.
    Customer, transaction, and marketing response databases hold
    different information, cover different (intersecting)
    populations, and vary in reliability.
  - Data costs vary: some nearly free, some take effort, some must
    be purchased, some do not exist and need an ancillary project
    to collect. Critical here: estimate cost vs. benefit per data
    source and decide whether further investment is merited. This
    is the "data as an asset with an acquisition cost" idea.
  - Even acquired data need collating. Customer records and product
    identifiers are notoriously noisy; matching records so each
    customer appears once is itself a hard analytics problem (book
    cites Hernandez & Stolfo 1995; Elmagarmid et al. 2007).
  - Data understanding can redirect the project or fork the team.
    Book's fraud example:
      - Credit card fraud: charges appear on the statement, so
        fraud gets caught (by company or customer). Fraudster and
        legitimate customer are different people with opposite
        goals, so labels (fraud / legitimate) are reliable. Classic
        supervised problem.
      - Medicare fraud (billions of dollars a year in the US):
        perpetrators (providers submitting false claims, sometimes
        patients) are also legitimate users of the billing system.
        No disinterested third party declares the correct charges,
        so no reliable target variable. Supervised learning does
        not apply; use unsupervised approaches (profiling,
        clustering, anomaly detection, co-occurrence grouping).
      - Lesson: "both are fraud detection" is a superficial,
        misleading similarity. Dig beneath the surface, uncover the
        structure of problem and data, then match to one or more
        tasks. A business problem often contains several tasks of
        different types whose solutions must be combined (book
        points to Ch. 11).
      - KEY TERM: supervised learning. Learning from data with a
        known target (label) per example, e.g. fraud / legitimate.
        (added definition)
      - KEY TERM: unsupervised learning. Finding structure in data
        with no target variable, e.g. clustering or anomaly
        detection. (added definition)
      - KEY TERM: target variable. The quantity or class the model
        is meant to predict. (added definition)
  - Victor's example: "We have customer demographics, subscription
    history, support calls, and cancellation records." His summary:
    determine what the data contain, whether they are relevant,
    and what problems they have.

  3.3 DATA PREPARATION
  - Analytic tools impose requirements on data form, so conversion
    is usually needed. Prep often runs alongside data understanding
    rather than strictly after it.
  - Typical work: convert to tabular format, remove or infer
    missing values, convert data types. Some techniques want
    symbolic / categorical data, others only numeric. Numeric
    values often need normalizing or scaling to be comparable.
    Rules of thumb exist; Ch. 3 covers the usual mining data
    format. The book otherwise does not focus on prep (cites Pyle
    1999 as a whole book on it).
      - KEY TERM: normalization / scaling. Rescaling numeric
        variables to a common range or spread so no variable
        dominates purely by its units. (added definition)
  - Sidebar NOTE: data scientists may spend much early time
    defining the variables used later. A main entry point for
    human creativity, common sense, and business knowledge.
    Solution quality often rests on how well analysts structure
    the problem and craft the variables (and it can be hard for
    them to admit it).
  - LEAKAGE (book cites Kaufman et al. 2012):
      - KEY TERM: leak. A variable in the historical data carrying
        information about the target that would NOT actually be
        available at decision time.
      - Example 1: predicting whether a web visitor will end the
        session or keep surfing. "Total pages visited in the
        session" is very predictive, but known only after the
        session ends, when the target is also known (Kohavi et al.
        2000).
      - Example 2: predicting whether a customer will be a "big
        spender." Categories of items bought, or worse the tax
        paid, are very predictive but unknown at decision time
        (Kohavi & Parekh 2003).
      - Why it bites here: prep is done after the fact from
        historical data, so leaks are easy to build in unnoticed.
        Ch. 14 has a hard-to-find real case.
  - Victor's example: "Remove duplicates, deal with missing values,
    and create a variable representing number of support calls in
    the last 90 days." His last item is exactly the book's "craft
    the variables" sidebar point. (added inference: support calls
    in the prior 90 days is known at decision time, so not a leak.)

  3.4 MODELING
  - Book defers modeling to the next several chapters. Here: the
    output of modeling is some model or pattern capturing
    regularities in the data.
  - The primary place data mining techniques are applied, and the
    part of the craft where the most science and technology can be
    brought to bear. Worth knowing what techniques and algorithms
    exist.
      - KEY TERM: model. A simplified representation of reality,
        here a formula or rule structure built from data that maps
        inputs to a prediction. (added definition)
  - Victor's notes only (book does not state it in this section):
    usually try multiple approaches rather than assume one
    algorithm will win. His example: "Train logistic regression,
    decision tree, and random forest models to predict
    cancellation."
      - KEY TERM: logistic regression. A linear model for
        classification that outputs class probabilities. (added
        definition)
      - KEY TERM: decision tree. A model that splits data with a
        sequence of if/then tests on attributes, ending in a
        prediction at each leaf. (added definition)
      - KEY TERM: random forest. An ensemble of many decision trees
        trained on random subsets of data and attributes, with
        predictions combined by voting or averaging. (added
        definition)

  3.5 EVALUATION
  - Purpose: assess results rigorously and gain confidence they are
    valid and reliable before moving on. Look hard enough at any
    dataset and you will find patterns, but they may not survive
    careful scrutiny; the aim is confidence that they are true
    regularities and not just idiosyncrasies or sample anomalies.
  - Lab vs. deployment: you can deploy straight from mining, but it
    is inadvisable. Testing first in a controlled laboratory
    setting is usually far easier, cheaper, quicker, and safer.
  - Equally important: evaluation checks that the model satisfies
    the ORIGINAL business goals (the primary goal of data science
    for business is to support decision making). The mining
    solution is usually one piece of a larger solution and must be
    judged as such.
  - A model can pass strict lab tests and still be impractical.
    Book's example: detection systems (fraud, spam, intrusion)
    often produce too many false alarms. A model can be more than
    99% accurate by lab standards and still be economically
    infeasible (cost of staff chasing alarms, cost of customer
    dissatisfaction).
      - KEY TERM: false alarm / false positive. The model flags an
        instance as positive (fraud, spam) when it is actually
        negative. (added definition)
      - KEY TERM: accuracy. Fraction of predictions that are
        correct. (added definition)
  - Evaluation is quantitative and qualitative. Stakeholders who
    sign off on deployment want to see the model does more good
    than harm and is unlikely to make catastrophic mistakes. So
    think about model comprehensibility, and if the model itself is
    not comprehensible (a complex formula), how to make its
    behavior comprehensible.
  - Why a thorough evaluation framework matters: detailed
    performance data on a deployed model can be hard or impossible
    to get. Production access is limited, and deployed systems have
    many moving parts, so isolating one part's contribution is
    hard. Sophisticated teams build testbeds mirroring production
    data as closely as possible before taking the deployment risk.
  - Extending evaluation beyond the lab (book's wording: "into the
    development environment"): instrument a live system for
    randomized experiments. Churn: after lab tests
    suggest the model reduces churn, run an "in vivo" evaluation
    where the live system randomly applies the model to some
    customers and holds others as a control group (ties back to
    Ch. 1 causal modeling). Design carefully; book points to Kohavi
    et al. (2007, 2009, 2012) for lessons learned.
      - KEY TERM: control group. Individuals deliberately NOT given
        the treatment (here, not scored / not targeted) so the
        treated group's outcome has a baseline. (added definition)
  - Also instrument deployed systems to make sure the world is not
    changing under the model. Behavior can change in direct
    response to deployment (fraud, spam), and input data can change
    in format or substance without anyone telling the data science
    team. Book cites Raeder et al. (2012) on designing for this.
  - Victor's example nails the business-goal half: "The model is
    92% accurate, but does it identify customers early enough that
    the company can actually do something?" His summary: does it
    solve the original business problem, not merely look good
    mathematically. Same emphasis as the book.

  3.6 DEPLOYMENT
  - Results of data mining (and increasingly the techniques
    themselves) are put into real use to realize a return on
    investment. Clearest case: implement a predictive model in an
    information system or business process. Churn: integrate the
    model with churn management, e.g. special offers to customers
    predicted high-risk. Fraud: build the model into a workforce
    management system that monitors accounts and opens "cases" for
    analysts.
  - Deploying the data mining SYSTEM rather than a fixed model:
    online ad targeting, where systems automatically build (and
    test) models in production when a new campaign appears. Two
    reasons:
      (i) the world may change faster than the data science team
          can adapt (fraud, intrusion detection);
      (ii) the business has too many modeling tasks to hand-curate
           each model.
    Then it is critical to instrument the process to alert the team
    to anomalies and provide fail-safe operation (Raeder et al.
    2012).
  - Sidebar NOTE, deployment can be low-tech: in a celebrated case,
    mining found rules that quickly diagnose and fix a common error
    in industrial printing. Deployment was taping a sheet of paper
    with the rules to the side of the printers (Evans & Fisher
    2002). Deployment can also be subtle: a change to data
    acquisition procedures, or a change in strategy, marketing, or
    operations driven by mining insight.
  - Victor's notes make the identical point: deployment does not
    necessarily mean an AI system in production. It can be
    integrating a model into software, producing a report or
    dashboard, or changing a business process.
  - Production re-coding: deploying usually means re-coding the
    model for the production environment (speed, compatibility),
    which can cost real money. Typically the data science team
    produces a working prototype plus its evaluation and hands both
    to a development team.
  - Sidebar NOTE, "over the wall" risk: the handoff from data
    science to development carries risk. Maxim: "Your model is not
    what the data scientists design, it's what the engineers
    build." Advice: bring developers in early as advisors;
    increasingly these are "data science engineers" (software
    engineers fluent in both production systems and data science).
    Developers gradually take on more responsibility and eventually
    own the product; data scientists stay involved through final
    deployment as advisors or developers depending on skill.

4. CLOSING THE LOOP
================================================================
  - Successful or not, deployment usually sends the process back to
    Business Understanding. Mining yields a lot of insight into the
    business problem and the difficulty of solving it, so a second
    iteration can produce a better solution. Thinking hard about
    the business, the data, and the performance goals often
    produces new ideas, even new lines of business or ventures.
  - You do not have to fail at deployment to restart. Evaluation
    may show results not good enough to deploy, requiring a changed
    problem definition or different data. That is the "shortcut"
    arrow from Evaluation to Business Understanding in Figure 2-2.
    In practice there should be shortcuts from every stage back to
    every prior one: the process always keeps some exploratory
    character and must be flexible enough to revisit earlier steps.
  - Victor's notes only, on where projects go wrong: the two phases
    people shortchange are 1 (Business Understanding) and 6
    (Deployment), and those are also the two that determine whether
    the project delivered anything. The book does not say this in
    one sentence, but it lines up with the book's emphasis on
    problem formulation as the key to success and on deployment as
    where return on investment is realized.
  - Tail of p. 16 opens "Implications for Managing the Data Science
    Team." Continues in Part 3.

================================================================
5. KEY TAKEAWAYS
================================================================
  - CRISP-DM = six stages: Business Understanding, Data
    Understanding, Data Preparation, Modeling, Evaluation,
    Deployment. Data sits at the center.
  - A cycle, not a line. Iteration is the rule; one pass is usually
    reconnaissance.
  - Most-used loops: Business <-> Data Understanding, Data Prep <->
    Modeling. Plus the Evaluation -> Business Understanding
    shortcut and the outer loop from Deployment.
  - Creativity lives in Business Understanding (problem
    formulation) and Data Preparation (crafting variables). Science
    and technology concentrate in Modeling.
  - Data is an asset with an acquisition cost; weigh cost vs.
    benefit per source. Superficially similar problems (credit card
    vs. Medicare fraud) can demand different task types because the
    data differ (reliable labels vs. none).
  - Leakage: a variable that predicts the target only because it
    would not be known until the target is. Watch for it because
    prep is done on historical data.
  - Evaluate in the lab first, then against the business goal, then
    (carefully) in vivo with a control group. High lab accuracy can
    still be economically infeasible (false alarms).
  - Deployment ranges from production code to a sheet of paper
    taped to a printer. "Over the wall" handoffs are risky; involve
    engineers early.
  - Underinvested stages (Victor's notes): Business Understanding
    and Deployment, the two that decide whether anything was
    delivered.
  - Managing it as a software project: Part 3.

6. QUIZ-SAFE FACTS
================================================================
  - CRISP-DM stands for Cross Industry Standard Process for Data
    Mining. Book citation: Shearer, 2000. Victor's notes only:
    dates from 1999; tool- and industry-agnostic.
  - Six stages in order: Business Understanding, Data
    Understanding, Data Preparation, Modeling, Evaluation,
    Deployment.
  - Figure 2-2 two-way arrows: Business Understanding <-> Data
    Understanding; Data Preparation <-> Modeling. Shortcut arrow:
    Evaluation -> Business Understanding.
  - Credit card fraud has reliable labels (supervised); Medicare
    fraud does not (unsupervised: profiling, clustering, anomaly
    detection, co-occurrence grouping).
  - Leak examples: total pages visited in a session (Kohavi et al.
    2000); item categories or tax paid for "big spender" (Kohavi &
    Parekh 2003). Leakage reference: Kaufman et al. 2012.
  - A detection model can exceed 99% accuracy in the lab and still
    fail on false-alarm cost in the business context.
  - Two reasons to deploy the mining system itself: the world
    changes faster than the team can adapt; too many modeling tasks
    to curate by hand.
  - Printer-rules deployment: rules taped to the side of the
    printers (Evans & Fisher 2002).
  - Maxim: "Your model is not what the data scientists design, it's
    what the engineers build."
  - Data science engineers: software engineers with expertise in
    both production systems and data science.
  - Evaluation-in-deployment and fail-safe design reference: Raeder
    et al. 2012. Randomized experiment lessons: Kohavi et al. 2007,
    2009, 2012.
