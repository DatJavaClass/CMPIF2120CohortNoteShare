1. CLOSING THE LOOP: BACK TO BUSINESS UNDERSTANDING
================================================================
  - "Over the wall" handoff risk: the model that ships is what
    the engineers build, not what the data scientists designed.
    Fix: bring development team members in early as advisors.
  - KEY TERM: data science engineer. A software engineer with
    expertise in both production systems and data science. Takes
    on more responsibility as the project matures and eventually
    owns the product; data scientists stay involved through final
    deployment as advisors or developers.
  - Whether or not deployment succeeds, the process often loops
    back to Business Understanding. Mining yields insight into
    the problem; a second iteration can improve the solution or
    even spawn new lines of business.
  - Deployment failure is not required to restart. Evaluation
    alone can show results are not good enough, forcing a new
    problem definition or new data. That is the "shortcut" arrow
    from Evaluation back to Business Understanding in the CRISP
    diagram. In practice every stage should have shortcuts back
    to every prior stage, because the process is exploratory.
  - KEY TERM: CRISP-DM (added definition). Cross Industry
    Standard Process for Data Mining: the six-stage cycle of
    Business Understanding, Data Understanding, Data Preparation,
    Modeling, Evaluation, Deployment, with arrows looping back.

================================================================
2. IMPLICATIONS FOR MANAGING THE DATA SCIENCE TEAM
================================================================
  - Tempting mistake: treating data mining as a software
    development cycle. Understandable: projects often start in
    software departments, data comes from a large system and
    results feed back into it, and software managers like agreed
    milestones and unambiguous success. The CRISP diagram looks
    deceptively like a dev cycle.
  - Why it is a mistake: data mining is exploratory, closer to
    R&D than engineering. CRISP iterates on approaches and
    strategy, not on software designs. Outcomes are far less
    certain, and one step's results can change the fundamental
    understanding of the problem.
  - Engineering straight for deployment is an expensive premature
    commitment. Instead buy information to reduce uncertainty:
      - Small: pilot studies, throwaway prototypes.
      - Literature review (what has been tried, how it worked).
      - Large: experimental testbeds for extensive agile
        experimentation.
  - To a software manager this looks like research, maybe more
    than they are comfortable with.

  SIDEBAR: SOFTWARE SKILLS VERSUS ANALYTICS SKILLS
  - Software engineering prizes efficient, high-quality code
    written from requirements; people get measured by code volume
    or bug tickets closed.
  - Analytics prizes: formulating problems well, prototyping
    quickly, making reasonable assumptions under ill-structured
    problems, designing experiments that are good investments,
    analyzing results.
  - Hire for the analytics list, not for traditional software
    engineering expertise, when building a data science team.

3. OTHER ANALYTICS TECHNIQUES: THE BIG PICTURE
================================================================
  - The book's focus is data-analytic thinking and extracting
    useful patterns. Other technologies sit beside it; know their
    goals, their role, and when to call in an expert.
  - The book says it presents "six groups" of related techniques;
    the section headings that follow are Statistics, Database
    Querying (incl. OLAP), Data Warehousing, Regression Analysis,
    Machine Learning and Data Mining, and a closing Answering
    Business Questions section (six headings). The dividing line:
    data mining is the AUTOMATED search for knowledge, patterns,
    or regularities in data. The others mostly are not.
  - Core business-analyst skill: recognizing which technique fits
    a given problem.

4. STATISTICS
================================================================
  - The word "statistics" has two senses in business analytics.

  Sense 1: summary statistics
  - KEY TERM: summary statistics. Catchall for computing numeric
    values of interest from data: sums, averages, rates, and so
    on. Often computed CONDITIONALLY on subsets (churn rate for
    men vs. women; high-income customers in the Northeast). Basic
    building blocks of data science.
  - Pick a summary statistic with attention to (a) the business
    problem and (b) the DISTRIBUTION of the data.
  - Worked example: 2004 Census Bureau Economic Survey. Mean U.S.
    income was over $60,000, but income is highly skewed (many
    make little, some make enormously much), so the mean misleads
    for policy. Median income (half make more, half less) was
    only $44,389.
  - KEY TERM: skewed distribution (added definition). A
    distribution whose values pile up on one side with a long
    tail on the other, so the mean gets pulled toward the tail
    and away from the typical value.
  - Two questions to ask before trusting any statistic: have you
    thought about the question you want answered, and have you
    considered the distribution and whether the statistic fits?

  Sense 2: Statistics, the field
  - KEY TERM: Statistics (proper noun). The field of study; a
    component of the larger field of Data Science. Covers data
    distributions and which summaries suit each, using data to
    test hypotheses, and estimating the uncertainty of
    conclusions. Many model and pattern extraction techniques in
    the book have roots here.
  - KEY TERM: hypothesis testing. Statistical procedure that asks
    what the chance is that an observed difference or pattern is
    just random variation. For data mining, it helps decide
    whether a pattern is a valid general regularity or a chance
    occurrence in one dataset.
  - Worked example: preliminary study shows Northeast churn of
    22.5% vs. 15% nationwide. Churn varies by region and time, so
    some difference is expected, but 1.5x the national rate
    looks unusually high. Hypothesis testing answers "what is
    the chance this is random variation?"
  - KEY TERM: confidence interval. Quantification of uncertainty
    as a range. Example: overall churn is 15%, and 95% of the
    time the rate is expected to fall between 13% and 17%.
  - The contrast: data mining is hypothesis GENERATION (can we
    find patterns at all?). Hypothesis TESTING should follow,
    generally on different data (Chapter 5). Data mining also
    produces numerical estimates, and confidence intervals on
    those are wanted too (revisited under evaluation).
  - The book stops there on basic statistics; introductory texts
    cover it properly.
  - KEY TERM: correlation. Two meanings, like "statistics".
    General-purpose: variation in one quantity tells us something
    about variation in another (e.g. indicators that correlate
    with later defection). Technical: a specific formula, e.g.
    linear correlation. Correlation is the jumping-off point for
    Chapter 3.

5. DATABASE QUERYING
================================================================
  - KEY TERM: query. A specific request for a subset of data, or
    for statistics about data, written in a technical language
    and posed to a database system.
  - Tools are usually frontends to database systems, built on SQL
    or a GUI.
      - KEY TERM: SQL (added definition). Structured Query
        Language, the standard language for asking relational
        databases for data.
      - KEY TERM: QBE (query-by-example). Named in the source as
        the GUI example; a form you fill in describing the records
        you want instead of writing SQL (added definition).
  - Example: if "profitable" can be defined operationally from
    items in the database, a query answers "Who are the most
    profitable customers in the Northeast?" and returns a list,
    possibly ranked. Fundamental difference from data mining:
    NO discovery of patterns or models.
  - Queries fit when the analyst already suspects an interesting
    subpopulation and wants to investigate it or confirm a
    hypothesis. Suspecting middle-aged Northeast men churn oddly:

    SELECT * FROM CUSTOMERS WHERE AGE > 45 and SEX='M' and DOMICILE = 'NE'

    The "*" retrieves all information about them from the
    CUSTOMERS table.
  - The mining direction, by contrast: data mining could PRODUCE
    that query. Examine prior customers who did and did not
    defect, discover that the segment "AGE > 45 and SEX male and
    DOMICILE Northeast-USA" is predictive of churn, then
    translate the pattern into SQL and let a query tool pull the
    matching records.
  - Query tools do real work: summary statistics over
    subpopulations, sorting, joining multiple related tables.
    Data scientists tend to get good at writing queries.
  - KEY TERM: OLAP (On-line Analytical Processing). An
    easy-to-use GUI for querying large data collections to
    support exploration. "On-line" means realtime answers.
    Unlike ad hoc SQL, the DIMENSIONS of analysis must be
    pre-programmed (e.g. sales volume by region and time); the
    analyst then drills down by clicking, dragging, and
    manipulating dynamic charts.
  - OLAP is manual or visual exploration only: no modeling, no
    automatic pattern finding. Unlike OLAP, data mining tools
    generally can incorporate new dimensions of analysis easily
    as part of the exploration. OLAP tools can be a useful
    complement to data mining tools for discovery from business
    data.

================================================================
6. DATA WAREHOUSING
================================================================
  - KEY TERM: data warehouse. A store that collects and coalesces
    data from across an enterprise, often from multiple
    transaction-processing systems that each have their own
    database. Analytical systems can read from it.
  - Warehousing is a FACILITATING technology for data mining, not
    a necessity: most data mining does not touch a warehouse.
    But firms that build one can apply mining more broadly and
    deeply.
  - Example: a warehouse that integrates sales, billing, and HR
    records can be mined for characteristic patterns of
    effective salespeople.

================================================================
7. REGRESSION ANALYSIS
================================================================
  - KEY TERM: regression analysis (added definition). The family
    of analytic methods, widely used in statistics and
    econometrics-based fields, for modeling how a numeric outcome
    depends on other variables. Source point: it shares core
    methods with this book but differs in EMPHASIS.
  - Classic regression analysis: explain a particular dataset
    (why did these customers churn?).
  - This book (data mining): extract patterns that GENERALIZE to
    other data, to improve a business process. Typically that
    means estimating or predicting values for cases NOT in the
    analyzed data (which not-yet-departed customers are best to
    target?).
  - Consequences of the predictive emphasis: time spent on
    testing patterns on new data to check generality, and on
    techniques that curb the tendency to find dataset-specific
    patterns that do not generalize to the population.
  - KEY TERM: explanatory vs. predictive modeling (the book names
    the pair; definition added). Explanatory modeling aims to account for the data
    you have; predictive modeling aims to do well on data you have
    not seen. Source point: the
    debate between them runs deep (beyond the book's scope).
    Techniques overlap heavily, but explanatory lessons do not
    all carry over, so a reader trained in regression may hit
    lessons that look new or even contradictory.
  - KEY TERM: overfitting (added definition). Fitting patterns
    that are specific to the training data rather than the
    population; the failure mode the "generalize to new data"
    emphasis is guarding against.

8. MACHINE LEARNING AND DATA MINING
================================================================
  - KEY TERM: machine learning methods. The collection of methods
    for extracting (predictive) models from data. Developed
    contemporaneously in several fields, most notably Machine
    Learning, Applied Statistics, and Pattern Recognition.
  - History: Machine Learning arose as a subfield of Artificial
    Intelligence, concerned with improving an intelligent
    agent's knowledge or performance over time from its
    experience in the world. That improvement often means
    analyzing environmental data and predicting unknowns, so the
    data-analysis side grew to dominate the field. As methods
    were deployed broadly, ML, Applied Statistics, and Pattern
    Recognition grew close and the boundaries blurred.
  - KEY TERM: Data Mining / KDD (Knowledge Discovery and Data
    Mining). Field that started as an offshoot of Machine
    Learning and remains closely linked to it. Both analyze data
    for useful patterns, share techniques and algorithms, and
    researchers move between the communities seamlessly.
  - Differences that remain:
      - ML covers many kinds of performance improvement, so it
        includes robotics and computer vision (not part of KDD),
        plus agency and cognition (how an agent uses learned
        knowledge to reason and act), which are not Data Mining
        concerns.
      - KDD spun off to focus on concerns raised by real-world
        applications. A decade and a half later it is still more
        application-oriented than ML; commercial and business
        research gravitates to KDD.
      - KDD cares about the WHOLE analytics process: data
        preparation, model learning, evaluation, and so on.

9. ANSWERING BUSINESS QUESTIONS WITH THESE TECHNIQUES
================================================================
  Four related but subtly different questions. Knowing the
  difference tells you which technology to use and which people
  to consult.

  1. Who are the most profitable customers?
     - If "profitable" is clearly definable from existing data:
       a straightforward DATABASE QUERY. A standard query tool
       pulls customer records, sortable by cumulative transaction
       amount or another operational profitability indicator.

  2. Is there really a difference between the profitable
     customers and the average customer?
     - A question about a conjecture or hypothesis ("there is a
       difference in value to the company"). STATISTICAL
       HYPOTHESIS TESTING confirms or disconfirms it and can give
       a probability or confidence bound that the difference is
       real. Typical result: the value of profitable customers is
       significantly different from the average, with probability
       < 5% that this is due to random chance.

  3. But who really are these customers? Can I characterize
     them?
     - Wanting common characteristics, not just a list.
       Individual characteristics come from DATABASE QUERYING,
       which also yields summary statistics. Deeper analysis
       finds what characteristics DIFFERENTIATE profitable from
       unprofitable customers: that is DATA SCIENCE, using data
       mining for automated pattern finding.

  4. Will some particular new customer be profitable? How much
     revenue should I expect this customer to generate?
     - DATA MINING techniques that examine historical customer
       records and build predictive models of profitability, then
       apply those models to new customers.
     - Two subtly different mining tasks. The first is
       CLASSIFICATION: predict whether a new customer will be
       profitable (yes/no, or the probability thereof). The
       second predicts a numerical value the customer will bring
       to the company. KEY TERM: regression (added definition).
       In data mining, the task of predicting a numeric value for
       each individual, as opposed to classification, which
       predicts a category.

10. CHAPTER SUMMARY
================================================================
  - Data mining is a craft with a well-defined process that
    raises the odds of success. The process is a conceptual tool
    the book keeps referring back to, showing where each
    fundamental concept fits.
  - Related fields developed canonical task types (classification,
    regression, clustering), each with its own purpose and
    solution techniques. A data scientist attacks a new project
    by decomposing it until one or more canonical tasks appear,
    choosing a technique for each, then composing the solutions.
    Doing this well takes experience.
  - A successful project is an intelligent compromise between
    what the data can do (what they can predict, and how well)
    and the project goals. Keep in mind how results will be used
    and let that inform the process.
  - Data mining differs from, and complements, supporting
    technologies like statistical hypothesis testing and database
    querying. Boundaries are not sharp, but knowing each
    technique's strengths tells you when to use it.
  - For a business manager, the process is a framework for
    analyzing a project or proposal: a set of questions to judge
    whether it is well conceived or fundamentally flawed.

================================================================
11. KEY TAKEAWAYS
================================================================
  - Data mining is R&D, not software engineering. Iterate on
    approaches and strategy; buy information (pilots, prototypes,
    lit review, testbeds) before committing to deployment.
  - Hire analysts for problem formulation, fast prototyping,
    sound assumptions, good experiment design, and analysis, not
    for lines of code.
  - Data mining = AUTOMATED pattern search. Querying and OLAP
    find what you already suspect; statistics tests whether it is
    real; mining finds it in the first place.
  - Mining generates hypotheses; statistics tests them, ideally
    on different data.
  - Match the statistic to the distribution (mean vs. median).
  - Regression analysis explains one dataset; predictive modeling
    must generalize to unseen cases. Same tools, different rules.
  - KDD grew out of ML; ML is broader (robotics, vision,
    agency), KDD is more application-oriented and process-wide.
  - Classification predicts a category; regression predicts a
    number.
