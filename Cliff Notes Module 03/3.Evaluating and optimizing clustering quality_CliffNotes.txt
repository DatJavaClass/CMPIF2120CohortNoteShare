1. WHY EVALUATE CLUSTERING QUALITY
  - There are multiple techniques for evaluating clustering
    quality. This lesson works with two metrics: the silhouette
    index and the within cluster sum of squares.
  - Why a metric is needed at all: k-means returns k clusters
    for whatever k you hand it, and nothing inside the
    algorithm reports whether those groups mean anything. A
    score is what lets you compare runs and choose k. (added
    explanation)
  - Running story for the worked examples here: a pizza shop is
    placing pickup lockers so every delivery address is near
    one. Each address is a point on the town grid (x = blocks
    east, y = blocks north), and each cluster gets one locker,
    sitting at that cluster's centroid. (added)
  - KEY TERM: centroid: the center of mass of a cluster, the
    average of its points coordinate by coordinate. See note 1
    of this module.
  - KEY TERM: Euclidean distance: straight line distance, the
    square root of the squared difference in x plus the squared
    difference in y. Every distance below is this. (added
    definition)

2. THE SILHOUETTE INDEX
  - KEY TERM: silhouette index (also called the silhouette
    coefficient): a widely used metric for evaluating the
    quality of clustering algorithms.
  - What it measures: how similar a data point is to its own
    cluster, which is cluster cohesion, compared to the other
    clusters, which is cluster separation.
  - KEY TERM: cohesion: how tightly the members of one cluster
    sit together. Tight is good. (added definition)
  - KEY TERM: separation: how far one cluster sits from the
    others. Far is good. (added definition)
  - It is computed one point at a time, from two quantities.
    a(i) is the average distance from point i to all the other
    points in the same cluster: the intracluster distance. In
    plain words, how far you are from your own crowd.
  - b(i) is the minimum average distance from point i to the
    points in the nearest neighboring cluster: the intercluster
    distance. Take each other cluster in turn, average the
    distance from i to every point in it, and keep the smallest
    of those averages. In plain words, how far you are from the
    nearest other crowd.
  - KEY TERM: intracluster distance: measured inside one
    cluster, from a point to its own cluster mates. (added
    definition)
  - KEY TERM: intercluster distance: measured between clusters,
    from a point to the members of a different cluster. (added
    definition)
  - The formula, with s(i) the silhouette score for point i:

      s(i) = (b(i) - a(i)) / max(a(i), b(i))

  - In words: the top is how much further the nearest other
    cluster is than your own. Positive means your own cluster
    is the closer of the two, which is what you want. Negative
    means the neighbors are closer than your own cluster mates.
    Dividing by the larger of the two distances pins the answer
    between -1 and 1 whatever the grid units are. (added
    explanation)
  - The range runs from -1 to +1:
      +1 means the point is well clustered, far from the other
        clusters.
      0 means the point is on the boundary of two clusters,
        the case where a(i) = b(i). (added last clause)
      -1 means the point may be in the wrong cluster.
  - The overall silhouette score is the average of s(i) across
    all data points: one number for the clustering, higher
    better. Being an average, it can hide a few badly placed
    points, so read the per point scores too. (added note)

3. SILHOUETTE BY HAND: SIX ADDRESSES, TWO CLUSTERS
  - Six delivery addresses: P1 (1,1), P2 (2,1), P3 (1,2),
    P4 (7,7), P5 (8,7), P6 (4,4). The clustering under test
    puts cluster A = P1, P2, P3 and cluster B = P4, P5, P6.
  - Every distance needed, rounded to three decimals:

          P1     P2     P3     P4     P5     P6
      P1  0      1      1      8.485  9.220  4.243
      P2  1      0      1.414  7.810  8.485  3.606
      P3  1      1.414  0      7.810  8.602  3.606
      P4  8.485  7.810  7.810  0      1      4.243
      P5  9.220  8.485  8.602  1      0      5
      P6  4.243  3.606  3.606  4.243  5      0

  - With two clusters the nearest neighboring cluster is always
    the other one, so b(i) is the average distance to all three
    points on the far side.
  - P1: a = (1 + 1)/2 = 1.000. b = (8.485 + 9.220 + 4.243)/3 =
    7.316. s = (7.316 - 1.000)/7.316 = 0.863.
  - P2: a = (1 + 1.414)/2 = 1.207. b = (7.810 + 8.485 +
    3.606)/3 = 6.634. s = 0.818.
  - P3: a = 1.207. b = (7.810 + 8.602 + 3.606)/3 = 6.673.
    s = 0.819.
  - P4: a = (1 + 4.243)/2 = 2.621. b = (8.485 + 7.810 +
    7.810)/3 = 8.035. s = 0.674.
  - P5: a = (1 + 5)/2 = 3.000. b = (9.220 + 8.485 + 8.602)/3 =
    8.769. s = 0.658.
  - P6: a = (4.243 + 5)/2 = 4.621. b = (4.243 + 3.606 +
    3.606)/3 = 3.818. s = (3.818 - 4.621)/4.621 = -0.174.
  - Overall score = the average of the six = 0.610.
  - Five points score high: tight with their own cluster mates
    and far from the other side. P6 at (4,4) is negative, the
    case the lecture calls a point that may be in the wrong
    cluster: on average it is closer to cluster A than to its
    own cluster B.
  - Move P6 into cluster A and score again: the per point
    values become 0.765, 0.754, 0.755, 0.859, 0.872, 0.174 and
    the overall score rises to 0.697. P6 is positive now but
    still small, because it genuinely sits between the groups.
    The metric found the better arrangement and also said P6
    will not be comfortable anywhere.
  - In Python it is two calls:

      import numpy as np
      from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_samples
      from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score
      arr_pts = np.array([[1,1],[2,1],[1,2],[7,7],[8,7],[4,4]])
      arr_lab = np.array([0,0,0,1,1,1]) ## cluster per point
      print(silhouette_samples(arr_pts, arr_lab).round(3))
      print(round(silhouette_score(arr_pts, arr_lab), 3)) ## 0.61

4. WITHIN CLUSTER SUM OF SQUARES
  - KEY TERM: within cluster sum of squares (WCSS): the sum of
    the squared distances between each point and the centroid
    of the cluster it belongs to.
  - The formula is a double sum: one sum over the clusters,
    one over the points inside each cluster.

      WCSS = sum over clusters, sum over the points in each
             cluster, of (point minus its centroid) squared

  - In words: for every cluster, and for every point within
    that cluster, take the difference between the data point
    and its centroid, square it, and add all of those up into
    one total. The squaring removes the sign and makes a far
    point count for much more than a near one. (added last
    sentence)
  - The intuition: a lower WCSS means the points are closer to
    their cluster centroid, so the clusters are more compact.
    Lower is better.
  - As the number of clusters increases, WCSS will typically
    decrease. More lockers means everyone is nearer to a
    locker, and at k equal to the number of points WCSS
    reaches exactly zero, since every point is then its own
    centroid: a perfect score that tells you nothing. (added
    last sentence)
  - However, with too many clusters you can have an overfit.
  - KEY TERM: overfit: a model fitted so closely to the data in
    front of it that it captures the noise along with the
    structure and stops describing anything general. Here it is
    a k so large that the clusters are the individual points
    back again. (added definition)
  - So WCSS cannot be minimized on its own to pick k. It has to
    be read against how fast it is still falling, which is the
    elbow method. (added explanation)
  - KEY TERM: inertia: the scikit-learn name for WCSS, exposed
    on a fitted KMeans object as the attribute inertia_. (added
    definition)

5. WCSS BY HAND: EIGHT ADDRESSES, TWO LOCKERS
  - Take the eight address clustering that note 1 of this
    module carries to convergence: addresses (1,1), (2,1),
    (1,2), (2,3) around a locker at (1.5, 1.75), and (6,5),
    (7,5), (6,6), (8,7) around a locker at (6.75, 5.75).
  - The double sum says do one cluster, then the other, then
    add the two totals.
  - Squared distance from each address to (1.5, 1.75):
      (1,1): 0.25 + 0.5625 = 0.8125
      (2,1): 0.25 + 0.5625 = 0.8125
      (1,2): 0.25 + 0.0625 = 0.3125
      (2,3): 0.25 + 1.5625 = 1.8125
      Cluster total: 3.75
  - Squared distance from each address to (6.75, 5.75):
      (6,5): 0.5625 + 0.5625 = 1.125
      (7,5): 0.0625 + 0.5625 = 0.625
      (6,6): 0.5625 + 0.0625 = 0.625
      (8,7): 1.5625 + 1.5625 = 3.125
      Cluster total: 5.5
  - WCSS = 3.75 + 5.5 = 9.25, the number scikit-learn reports
    as inertia_ for the same fit. No square roots appear: the
    metric stays in squared distance, so only the comparison
    between totals carries meaning. (added note)

6. THE ELBOW METHOD
  - The problem it solves: determining the best k for k-means.
  - The procedure:
      Identify a quality metric. Here it is the within cluster
        sum of squares.
      Perform the clustering for different values of k, that
        is, repeat k-means for many k values.
      Plot the quality metric on the y axis and the k value on
        the x axis.
      Visually identify the point of diminishing returns for
        increasing k. After some point, k can be increased
        without adding much to the quality.
  - KEY TERM: point of diminishing returns: the k past which
    each additional cluster buys a much smaller improvement
    than the clusters before it did. (added definition)
  - The name: the curve drops steeply, then flattens, and the
    corner between the two looks like the elbow of an arm being
    extended a little. That bend is the k you pick.

7. THE LECTURE'S TEN POINT WALK
  - The lecture runs a small dataset of 10 data points through
    k-means for every k from 1 to 10.
  - k = 1 is trivial: everything is a single cluster. k = 2
    gives two clusters, one holding all the top left points and
    one holding all the bottom right points.
  - k = 3 splits the points into three clusters, and visual
    inspection says that is reasonable. k = 4, k = 5, and k = 6
    split things a little further each time and still seem
    reasonable.
  - Past 7 you can tell that points which were close to each
    other are being split apart, and the same holds at 8 and 9.
    At k = 10 every point is its own cluster.
  - The plot puts WCSS on the y axis, where lower is better,
    and the number of clusters on the x axis, from 1 to 10. It
    shows one big drop and after that things are negligibly
    better. From 1 to 2 the drop is really big, and from there
    it slows.
  - The lecture reads the elbow at k = 2. Beyond it you get
    some improvement, but you are effectively at the point of
    diminishing returns, so it is not really worth it. k = 2 is
    the best choice for that data.

8. AN ELBOW FROM END TO END
  - Ten delivery addresses: (1,1), (2,2), (1,3), (8,1), (9,2),
    (8,3), (4,8), (5,9), (6,8), (5,5). By eye there is a
    southwest group, a southeast group, a north group, and one
    address at (5,5) sitting in the middle of town. These ten
    addresses are the notes' own, not the lecture's ten points,
    so the elbow lands somewhere else.
  - Run k-means for k = 1 to 10 and record the WCSS:
      k = 1    162.500
      k = 2     87.238
      k = 3     16.333
      k = 4      8.000
      k = 5      6.333
      k = 6      4.667
      k = 7      3.000
      k = 8      2.000
      k = 9      1.000
      k = 10     0.000
  - k = 1 checks by hand: one locker at the grand mean of all
    ten addresses, (4.9, 4.2), with every address measured to
    it. Those squared distances add to 162.5. At k = 10 every
    address is its own locker and every distance is zero.
  - Now read the drops. From 1 to 2 removes 75.262. From 2 to 3
    removes another 70.905. From 3 to 4 removes only 8.333, and
    every step after that buys a few units at most.
  - The elbow is at k = 3. The second and third lockers pay for
    themselves; the fourth does not. All the fourth does is
    make the address at (5,5) a cluster of one, the overfit
    from section 4 arriving on schedule.
  - The silhouette index agrees, which is the check worth
    running. On the same runs: k = 2 gives 0.446, k = 3 gives
    0.678, k = 4 gives 0.545, k = 5 gives 0.376, k = 6 gives
    0.249, k = 7 gives 0.088, k = 8 gives 0.059, k = 9 gives
    0.029. It peaks at k = 3, the answer the elbow gave.
  - The silhouette is undefined at k = 1 (no other cluster, so
    no b) and at k equal to the number of points (nobody else
    in your cluster, so no a), so it is computed only for k
    from 2 up to n minus 1. (added explanation)
  - The code, and note that a fitted KMeans hands back the WCSS
    directly:

      import numpy as np
      from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
      from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score
      arr_pts = np.array([[1,1],[2,2],[1,3],[8,1],[9,2],
                          [8,3],[4,8],[5,9],[6,8],[5,5]])
      for k in range(1, 11):
          km = KMeans(n_clusters=k, n_init=10,
                      random_state=0).fit(arr_pts)
          s = float("nan") ## undefined at both ends
          if 1 < k < 10:
              s = silhouette_score(arr_pts, km.labels_)
          print(k, round(km.inertia_, 3), round(s, 3))

9. KEY TAKEAWAYS
  - Silhouette weighs cohesion against separation one point at
    a time: a(i) measures you against your own cluster mates,
    b(i) against the nearest other cluster, and the clustering
    gets the mean of the per point scores.
  - A negative per point silhouette is actionable: it names the
    point to move, and moving it raised the overall score from
    0.610 to 0.697 in the six address example.
  - WCSS typically falls as k rises and reaches zero when
    every point is its own cluster, so its minimum is
    worthless as a target. What you read is the rate of the
    fall.
  - The elbow method finds that rate change on a plot: the bend
    where extra clusters stop paying for themselves.
  - In scikit-learn: km.inertia_ is WCSS, silhouette_score is
    the overall silhouette, silhouette_samples the per point
    silhouette.

10. QUIZ-SAFE FACTS
  - Multiple techniques exist for evaluating clustering
    quality; this lesson covers two, the silhouette index and
    the within cluster sum of squares.
  - The silhouette index is also called the silhouette
    coefficient and is widely used.
  - It measures how similar a data point is to its own cluster,
    that is cluster cohesion, compared to other clusters, which
    implies cluster separation.
  - a(i) is the average distance to all other points in the
    same cluster, the intracluster distance. b(i) is the
    minimum average distance to points in the nearest
    neighboring clusters, the intercluster distance.
  - s(i) = (b(i) - a(i)) / max(a(i), b(i)), and the overall
    silhouette score is the average of s(i) across all points.
  - Silhouette values range from -1 to +1: +1 means the point
    is well clustered and far from other clusters, 0 means it
    is on the boundary of two clusters, -1 means it may be in
    the wrong cluster.
  - WCSS is the sum of squared distances between each point and
    the centroid of the cluster it belongs to, written as a
    double sum over the clusters and over the points in each.
  - Lower WCSS means points are closer to their cluster
    centroid, so the clusters are more compact.
  - As the number of clusters increases, WCSS will typically
    decrease, and too many clusters can produce an overfit.
  - Elbow method steps for k-means: identify a quality metric
    (the lecture uses WCSS), perform clustering for different
    values of k, plot the metric on the y axis against k on the
    x axis, and visually identify the point of diminishing
    returns.
  - The lecture's example has 10 data points run for k = 1
    through k = 10; k = 1 is a single cluster, k = 2 gives a
    top left cluster and a bottom right cluster, and at k = 10
    every point is its own cluster.
  - On that plot the drop from 1 to 2 is really big and after
    that the improvement is negligible.
  - The elbow gets its name from the bend resembling the elbow
    of a partly extended arm.
  - The lecture concludes that k = 2 is the best choice for
    that dataset.
