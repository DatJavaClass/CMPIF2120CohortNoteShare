1. WHERE THIS LESSON PICKS UP
  - This lesson is all examples, harder than the first lesson's.
    The professor takes one set of 10 points and runs k-means
    three times: k = 3 from one set of starting centroids, k = 3
    again from another, then k = 2. See note 1 of this module
    for the algorithm and for hierarchical clustering.
  - The algorithm in four lines (recap): pick k starting
    centroids, assign every point to the centroid nearest it,
    recompute each centroid as the average of the points now
    assigned to it, repeat that pair until nothing moves.
  - KEY TERM: cluster: a group of points the algorithm has
    decided belong together. The lecture gives each one a color.
    (added definition)
  - KEY TERM: centroid: the center of a cluster, the average of
    the coordinates of its points. It is a location, usually not
    one of the data points after the first update. (added
    definition)
  - KEY TERM: k: the number of clusters, chosen before the run.
    (added definition)
  - Where the first centroids come from: typically, when we
    implement k-means, we pick them out of the existing points.
    Good centroids are very difficult to pick with no idea where
    the data are, and picking from the points you have
    guarantees they land inside the space the points occupy.
    Every example here uses that technique.
  - KEY TERM: seed: the set of starting centroids a run begins
    from. (added definition)
  - KEY TERM: Euclidean distance: straight line distance,
    sqrt((x1 - x2)^2 + (y1 - y2)^2) in 2-D. This is what
    "closest" means throughout. (added definition)

2. THE LECTURE'S THREE RUNS ON TEN POINTS
  - The data: 10 points generated from a script. All three runs
    draw their initial centroids from those points, and section
    9 lists the assignments each run made.
  - Run 1, k = 3 seeded at points 2, 4 and 9. Every other point
    joins the nearest of the three, the three clusters are
    labelled red, green and purple, then each centroid moves to
    its cluster's average. The second pass changes no
    assignment, so the new centroids match the old: convergence,
    and that is where we stop.
  - KEY TERM: convergence: another pass changes neither the
    assignments nor the centroids, so the algorithm stops.
  - KEY TERM: iteration: one full pass, assign then recompute.
    (added definition)
  - Run 2, k = 3 seeded at points 1, 6 and 8, chosen to produce
    a little movement between clusters. Point 10 starts purple
    and changes affiliation to green on the second assignment,
    the two centroids having shifted under it. One more pass
    changes nothing: convergence, on pretty much the same
    groups.
  - The center of gravity picture: think of the points as balls
    on a tray. The centroid is the exact spot where you could
    hold the tray and it would not topple over. In run 3 the
    professor makes them beers on a tray you have to balance.
  - KEY TERM: center of mass (center of gravity): the balance
    point of a set of objects. For equal weights it is the plain
    average of their coordinates, which is what the centroid
    update computes. (added definition)
  - Run 3, k = 2 seeded at points 2 and 9: three groups the
    naked eye can see, squeezed into two clusters. The green
    center moves up significantly to average the points it now
    holds, and the red center moves up and to the right to
    counterbalance 1, 2 and 5 on one side against 10 on the
    other. Point 10 then changes teams from red to green, the
    red centroid moves inside the triangle formed by points 1, 2
    and 5, the green centroid moves left, and the pass after
    that brings no changes: convergence.

3. WORKED EXAMPLE: TEN ADDRESSES, THREE LOCKERS
  - The running example for this module: a pizza shop wants k
    pickup lockers placed so every delivery address is close to
    one. An address is a point on the town grid, x blocks east
    and y blocks north, and each locker goes at the centroid of
    the addresses it serves.
  - The ten addresses:
      1 (1,1)   2 (2,2)   3 (1,3)
      4 (8,1)   5 (9,2)   6 (8,3)
      7 (4,8)   8 (5,9)   9 (6,8)
      10 (5,5)
  - By eye: a southwest group (1, 2, 3), a southeast group
    (4, 5, 6), a north group (7, 8, 9), and address 10 alone in
    the middle of the map.
  - The seed: k = 3, centroids taken from the addresses as the
    lecture prescribes, addresses 1, 4 and 7 becoming red, green
    and purple.
  - Iteration 1, assignment. Each address goes to the nearest of
    (1,1), (8,1) and (4,8). Red takes 1, 2, 3. Green takes 4, 5,
    6. Purple takes 7, 8, 9 and 10.
      Address 10 at (5,5) is the one worth checking. To (1,1):
      sqrt(16 + 16) = 5.657. To (8,1): sqrt(9 + 16) = 5.0. To
      (4,8): sqrt(1 + 9) = 3.162. Purple, so the lonely middle
      address is served by the north locker.
  - Iteration 1, recompute. Average the x values and the y
    values in each cluster:
      Red: ((1 + 2 + 1)/3, (1 + 2 + 3)/3) = (1.333, 2.0).
      Green: ((8 + 9 + 8)/3, (1 + 2 + 3)/3) = (8.333, 2.0).
      Purple: ((4 + 5 + 6 + 5)/4, (8 + 9 + 8 + 5)/4)
        = (5.0, 7.5).
  - Iteration 2: assign again against those centroids. Every
    address keeps its cluster, so the recomputed centroids are
    identical. Converged on the second pass. Lockers at (1.333,
    2.0), (8.333, 2.0) and (5.0, 7.5). Address 10 has pulled the
    purple locker south of the north group, since nothing in
    k-means lets a point opt out. (added note)

4. THE SAME ADDRESSES FROM A WORSE SEED
  - Same addresses, same k = 3, centroids at addresses 10, 4 and
    8. The awkward case: the lonely middle address is a seed.
  - Iteration 1, assignment. Red (from address 10 at (5,5))
    takes 1, 2, 3 and itself. Green (from address 4) takes 4, 5,
    6. Purple (from address 8) takes 7, 8, 9.
      Check address 2 at (2,2). To (5,5): sqrt(9 + 9) = 4.243.
      To (8,1): sqrt(36 + 1) = 6.083. To (5,9): sqrt(9 + 49) =
      7.616. Red, so the southwest group hangs off the middle
      address.
  - Iteration 1, recompute:
      Red: ((1 + 2 + 1 + 5)/4, (1 + 2 + 3 + 5)/4)
        = (2.25, 2.75).
      Green: (8.333, 2.0).
      Purple: ((4 + 5 + 6)/3, (8 + 9 + 8)/3) = (5.0, 8.333).
  - Iteration 2, assignment: address 10 changes teams. To the
    red centroid (2.25, 2.75) it is sqrt(7.5625 + 5.0625) =
    3.553, to the purple centroid (5.0, 8.333) it is 3.333.
    Purple wins by about two tenths of a block. Red keeps 1, 2,
    3, purple holds 7, 8, 9, 10. The point did not move, the
    centroid chasing it did.
  - Iteration 2, recompute: red back to (1.333, 2.0), green
    still (8.333, 2.0), purple (5.0, 7.5). Iteration 3: nothing
    changes, converged. Identical lockers to section 3, one
    extra iteration to reach them. A worse seed does not always
    end this politely, which is why section 7's choices matter.
    (added note)

5. TWO LOCKERS FOR THREE NEIGHBORHOODS
  - Same addresses, k = 2, centroids at addresses 1 and 4.
    Three visible groups plus a straggler, two lockers.
  - Iteration 1, assignment. Red (from (1,1)) takes 1, 2, 3 and
    7. Green (from (8,1)) takes 4, 5, 6, 8, 9 and 10.
      The north group gets split. Address 7 (4,8) is 7.616
      from (1,1) and 8.062 from (8,1), so red. Address 8 (5,9)
      is 8.944 from (1,1) and 8.544 from (8,1), so green. Two
      addresses one block apart on each axis land on opposite
      teams, the only comparison being distance to the two
      seeds.
  - Iteration 1, recompute:
      Red: ((1 + 2 + 1 + 4)/4, (1 + 2 + 3 + 8)/4) = (2.0, 3.5).
      Green: ((8 + 9 + 8 + 5 + 6 + 5)/6,
        (1 + 2 + 3 + 9 + 8 + 5)/6) = (6.833, 4.667).
  - Iteration 2, assignment: address 7 changes teams to green,
    4.924 to the red centroid against 4.374 to the green one.
    Red keeps 1, 2, 3, green holds the rest.
  - Iteration 2, recompute: red (1.333, 2.0), green (45/7, 36/7)
    = (6.429, 5.143). Iteration 3: no changes, converged.
  - What k = 2 did to the map: the north group and the southeast
    group were welded into one cluster whose locker at (6.429,
    5.143) sits in empty space between them. Forcing three
    natural groups into two does not split the difference, it
    parks a center where nobody lives. The spread of the
    addresses around their lockers rises from 16.333 with three
    lockers to 87.238 with two, and note 3 of this module
    defines that number and uses it to pick k. (added note)

6. THE WORKED EXAMPLE IN PYTHON
  - scikit-learn does the whole loop in one call:

      import numpy as np
      from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
      arr_pts = np.array([[1,1],[2,2],[1,3],[8,1],[9,2],[8,3],
                          [4,8],[5,9],[6,8],[5,5]])
      km = KMeans(n_clusters=3, n_init=10,
                  random_state=0).fit(arr_pts) ## three lockers
      print(km.labels_) ## cluster id per address
      print(km.cluster_centers_.round(3)) ## locker sites

  - It prints labels [2 2 2 0 0 0 1 1 1 1] and centers (8.333,
    2.0), (5.0, 7.5), (1.333, 2.0): the three lockers of section
    3, in label order. The ids are only names, so which group is
    0 carries no meaning. Read the labels as who shares a
    locker: 1, 2, 3, then 4, 5, 6, then 7, 8, 9 and 10.
  - n_init=10 runs the algorithm ten times from ten different
    seeds and keeps the best result, which is how the library
    defends against the seed of section 4. random_state=0 fixes
    those seeds so the call always prints the same thing. (added
    explanation)
  - Set n_clusters=2 for section 5 and the centers come back as
    (6.429, 5.143) and (1.333, 2.0). (added note)

7. PICKING THE INITIAL CENTROIDS
  - The one thing obvious in all of these examples is that the
    assignment of the initial centroids is very crucial. Three
    approaches, in the professor's labels:
      Expired: pick completely random points. Not used anymore.
        The chances of a good initial assignment are slim, and
        it requires knowing a lot about the domain and the point
        space.
      Wired: pick k random points out of the existing set of
        points. The current standard approach for k-means. It
        guarantees you do not pick points nowhere near any
        existing points, but it is still sensitive to which
        points get chosen.
      Upgraded: k-means++. Pick the first point completely at
        random from the set of points. Centroids 2, 3, up to k
        come from a probability distribution favoring points
        farther away from those already picked: after the random
        first, the second is effectively the remaining point
        farthest from it, and three and four go the same way.
        From there it gets a little more mathy.
  - KEY TERM: k-means++: that scheme, random first pick then
    distance weighted picks. It is scikit-learn's default for
    init, which is why the code above did not ask for it. (added
    note)
  - The closing summary: k-means is simple, very understandable,
    and assigns items to clusters automatically. Its
    disadvantages: the number of clusters must be picked
    beforehand, and all items are forced into clusters, which
    can make it too sensitive to outliers.
  - KEY TERM: outlier: a point far from every group, like
    address 10 in the worked example. k-means has no "none of
    the above" option, so an outlier joins some cluster and
    pulls its centroid toward itself. (added definition)

8. KEY TAKEAWAYS
  - Initial centroids are normally chosen from the existing data
    points, which keeps them inside the space the data occupies.
  - Convergence is the stopping rule: a pass that changes
    neither the assignments nor the centroids ends the run.
  - A point changes teams because the centroids moved under it,
    not because the point moved. Two of the lecture's runs and
    sections 4 and 5 turn on that.
  - A different seed can change the number of iterations without
    changing the final clusters (sections 3 and 4), and it can
    change the result too, which is why k-means++ exists.
  - Choosing k too small merges natural groups and parks a
    centroid where no points are.

9. QUIZ-SAFE FACTS
  - Typically, when implementing k-means, centroids are picked
    out of the existing points, because good centroids are hard
    to pick without knowing where the data are and this
    guarantees they are within the space of the points.
  - The examples use 10 points from a script, run with two
    different seeds for the initial centroids and then with a
    changed value of k.
  - First run: k = 3 with centroids at points 2, 4 and 9. Point
    2 got 1 and 5, point 9 got 8 and 7, point 4 got 3, 6 and 10.
    The purple cluster started as points 8, 7 and 9, and its
    centroid moved off point 9 to their average.
  - The first run converged on the second pass: assignments
    unchanged and new centroids identical to the old.
    Convergence means the centroids and the assignments do not
    change, and that is where the algorithm stops.
  - Second run: same points, centroids at 1, 6 and 8. Point 1
    was closest to 2 and 5, point 8 to 7 and 9, point 6 to 3 and
    4, and point 10 came out closer to 8 than to 6. Point 10
    then changed affiliation from purple to green, because the
    green centroid moved down a little and the purple centroid
    moved up only slightly.
  - The centroid is the center of gravity: if the points were
    balls on a tray, it is the spot to hold the tray so it does
    not topple over.
  - Third run: k = 2 with centroids at points 2 and 9, forcing
    data that looks like three clusters to the naked eye into
    two. Point 2 initially took 1, 5 and 10, point 9 took 8, 7,
    3, 4 and 6 but not 10, and point 10 then changed teams from
    red to green.
  - In the third run the green center moved up significantly and
    the red center moved up and to the right, each balancing the
    points it had to average.
  - The assignment of initial centroids is very crucial.
    Expired approach: completely random points, not used, the
    chance of a good initial assignment is slim. Wired approach:
    k random points out of the existing set, the current
    standard, still sensitive to which points are chosen.
    Upgraded approach: k-means++, first centroid random and the
    rest from a probability distribution favoring points farther
    from those already picked.
  - k-means advantages: simple, very understandable, assigns
    items to clusters automatically. Disadvantages: the number
    of clusters must be picked beforehand, all items are forced
    into clusters, and it can be too sensitive to outliers.
  - The professor's wrap up: k-means was shown with one very
    simple example and three slightly more sophisticated ones.
