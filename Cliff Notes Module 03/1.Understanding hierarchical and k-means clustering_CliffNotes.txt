1. WHAT CLUSTERING IS
  - Clustering is the task of discovering groups and
    structures in the data that are in some way similar to
    each other. Members of a cluster should be more alike
    among each other than they are to members of other
    clusters.
  - KEY TERM: cluster: a group of data points held together
    because they resemble each other more than anything
    outside the group.
  - Given a scatter of points, the lecture slide shows the eye
    picking out three groups with nothing to go on but the
    layout. That is the intuition a clustering algorithm
    automates. (last sentence added)
  - Clustering is one type of unsupervised learning.
  - KEY TERM: unsupervised learning: modeling on data with no
    labels and no known answer to score against. Nobody tells
    the algorithm what the right groups are, so it finds
    structure on its own. (added definition)

2. TYPES OF CLUSTERING ALGORITHMS
  - Two families: hierarchical and point assignment.
  - KEY TERM: hierarchical clustering: algorithms that build
    clusters by repeatedly merging or splitting them, in two
    subcategories.
  - KEY TERM: agglomerative: the bottom up one. Initially
    each point is a cluster by itself, and the two nearest
    clusters are repeatedly combined into one.
  - KEY TERM: divisive: the top down one. Start with
    everything in one cluster and recursively split it.
  - KEY TERM: point assignment: the algorithm maintains a set
    of clusters at any given time, and each point belongs to
    the cluster nearest to it. That membership can change
    over time until convergence.
  - Point assignment algorithms are the more popular type.
    k-means is the one this module works with. The
    difference: hierarchical algorithms never take a merge
    back, while point assignment keeps a working answer and
    lets points change hands. (last sentence added)

3. THE THREE QUESTIONS OF HIERARCHICAL CLUSTERING
  - The key operation, in the agglomerative case at least, is
    to repeatedly combine the two nearest clusters. Three
    questions have to be answered first: how do you represent
    a cluster of more than one point, how do you determine
    the nearness of clusters, and when do you stop combining
    clusters?
  - Answer 1: comparing clusters efficiently needs one
    representative per cluster, and in the simpler case, using
    Euclidean distance, that representative is the centroid.
  - KEY TERM: point, on a 2-D plane: one data record drawn as
    a dot at its two feature values, x across and y up. Every
    example in this module lives on such a plane. (added
    definition)
  - KEY TERM: centroid: the average of all the data points in
    a cluster. Average the x values, average the y values,
    and that pair is the centroid. The professor's second way
    to think about it: the centroid is the center of mass, as
    if the points were objects laid out on a plane.
  - KEY TERM: Euclidean distance: ordinary straight line
    distance. Subtract the x values, subtract the y values,
    square both differences, add, take the square root:
    sqrt((x1 - x2)^2 + (y1 - y2)^2). (added definition)
  - Answer 2: nearness between clusters is the distance
    between their centroids. One number per pair, however many
    points each cluster holds. (second sentence added
    restatement)
  - Answer 3: bottom up stops when all points have been
    combined into one cluster. Top down stops when all
    clusters have been split into single points. Both run to
    the extreme, so what you keep is the sequence of merges,
    cut where the number of clusters suits you. (last
    sentence added)

4. THE K-MEANS ALGORITHM
  - k-means is the most popular clustering algorithm, popular
    because it is very simple. It works with numerical data
    only.
  - KEY TERM: k: the number of clusters to generate. You
    specify it before the algorithm runs.
  - The steps as the lecture gives them:
      Step 1. Pick k cluster centers at random. These are the
        initial cluster centers.
      Step 2. Assign every item, every point, to its nearest
        cluster center, for example using Euclidean distance.
      Step 3. Move each cluster center to the new mean of its
        assigned items.
      Step 4. Repeat the assignment and the recomputation
        until convergence.
  - KEY TERM: initial centroids, also called the seed: the
    starting positions of the k centers. (added definition)
  - KEY TERM: iteration: one full pass of assign then
    recompute. (added definition)
  - KEY TERM: convergence: the point where the change in
    cluster assignment is less than a threshold, ideally
    zero. Nothing moves and nothing changes hands, so another
    pass produces the same picture.
  - You may also want a timeout parameter: after a set number
    of iterations you stop, expecting little improvement
    beyond that point.

5. THE LECTURE'S EIGHT POINT WALK
  - Setup: a 2-D plane with eight orange points and two
    random initial centroids, green and blue.
  - The shortcut for the assignment step: draw the line
    connecting the two centroids and a dashed line through
    its exact middle, perpendicular to it. Everything on the
    green side is closer to the green centroid, everything on
    the other side to the blue one, so no distances are
    computed one at a time.
  - KEY TERM: perpendicular bisector: that dashed line, every
    position on it the same distance from both centroids.
    (added definition)
  - First assignment: three points green, five blue. Each
    centroid then becomes the average of the points carrying
    its color, moving off its random spot.
  - Reassign: the bottom green point used to be blue, but the
    green center is now much closer, so it changes. The
    professor calls this a team reassignment. The recomputed
    centroids fall squarely in the middle of the four green
    points and the four blue points.
  - Repeat once more: no point changes color. No changes
    means convergence, and the algorithm terminates.

6. WORKED EXAMPLE: WHERE TO PUT TWO PIZZA LOCKERS
  - The story for the worked examples in this module: a pizza
    shop wants to place k pickup lockers so every delivery
    address is close to one. An address is a point on the
    town grid, x blocks east and y blocks north, a locker
    goes at the centroid of its cluster, distances Euclidean.
  - Eight addresses, numbered 1 to 8:
    (1,1) (2,1) (1,2) (2,3) (6,5) (7,5) (6,6) (8,7).
    Two lockers, so k = 2.
  - Initial centroids, picked from the addresses themselves:
    L1 = address 1 at (1,1), L2 = address 4 at (2,3).
  - Iteration 1, assignment. Address 4 is L2, so its distance
    is 0 and it goes to L2. Address 5 at (6,5): to L1 the
    gaps are 5 east and 4 north, so sqrt(25 + 16) = 6.403;
    to L2 the gaps are 4 and 2, so sqrt(16 + 4) = 4.472. L2
    is closer. Working through the rest the same way, L1 gets
    addresses 1, 2 and 3, and L2 gets 4, 5, 6, 7 and 8.
  - Iteration 1, new centroids. L1 is the average of (1,1),
    (2,1) and (1,2): x is (1 + 2 + 1) / 3 and y is
    (1 + 1 + 2) / 3, so L1 = (1.333, 1.333). L2 is the
    average of (2,3), (6,5), (7,5), (6,6) and (8,7): x is
    29 / 5 and y is 26 / 5, so L2 = (5.8, 5.2).
  - Iteration 2, assignment. Address 4 at (2,3) changes
    teams. Its distance to the new L1 is sqrt(0.444 + 2.778)
    = 1.795, and to the new L2 it is sqrt(14.44 + 4.84) =
    4.391. L1 now has 1, 2, 3 and 4, and L2 has 5, 6, 7 and
    8. Address 4 was a centroid a moment ago and now belongs
    to the other cluster, because L2 was dragged northeast by
    the five points around it. (last sentence added
    explanation)
  - Iteration 2, new centroids. L1 = (6 / 4, 7 / 4) =
    (1.5, 1.75). L2 = (27 / 4, 23 / 4) = (6.75, 5.75).
  - Iteration 3. Every address is still nearest the same
    locker and neither centroid moves. That is convergence
    and the run stops. The lockers go at (1.5, 1.75) and
    (6.75, 5.75).
  - Address 4 switching sides on the second pass is the whole
    mechanism in miniature: a point between two groups follows
    whichever center ends up nearer once the centers move to
    where the mass is. (added explanation)
  - Adding up how far each address sits from its locker, in
    squared distance, gives 9.25 at convergence: 3.75 for the
    west locker's four addresses and 5.5 for the east one's.
    That total is the within cluster sum of squares, and note
    3 of this module defines it and uses it to pick k. (added
    note)

7. PICKING THE INITIAL CENTROIDS
  - The professor's first summary point: the assignment of
    the initial centroids is very crucial. Had the initial
    points been picked elsewhere, the run would have had a
    different outcome, or at least different steps to the
    same outcome.
  - Approach 1, labeled expired: pick completely random
    points anywhere in the point space, as the eight point
    walk did purely as illustration. Not really used.
  - Approach 2, labeled wired: pick k random points from the
    existing set of points. Out of ten points, if k is two,
    you pick two of those ten. This is what is typically used
    and is the standard k-means approach. Starting from points
    that exist leaves no centroid stranded in an empty region
    of the space. (last sentence added)
  - Approach 3, labeled upgraded, is k-means++. Pick one
    random point from the set, then choose the remaining
    centroids from a probability distribution that favors
    points farther away from those already picked. For k = 2
    the second is essentially the point at the highest
    distance from the first. This explores the space more and
    is shown to be a little bit better.
  - KEY TERM: k-means++: the name of that scheme. The loop
    after it is ordinary k-means, only the choice of starting
    centers changes. (added definition)

8. ADVANTAGES AND DISADVANTAGES OF K-MEANS
  - Advantages: it is simple, it is very easy to understand,
    and it assigns items to clusters automatically.
  - Disadvantage: you have to pick the number of clusters
    beforehand. The k value is crucial and you must have it
    before you cluster.
  - Disadvantage: all items are forced into clusters, whether
    they would like that or not. As a result k-means is too
    sensitive to outliers: an obvious outlier still ends up
    in some cluster, and that may skew your results.
  - KEY TERM: outlier: a point sitting far from the rest of
    the data. Since a centroid is an average, one far point
    pulls its centroid away from the group it represents.
    (added definition)

9. KEY TAKEAWAYS
  - Clustering finds groups of similar points with no labels
    to learn from. It is unsupervised learning.
  - Hierarchical: agglomerative merges the two nearest
    clusters bottom up, divisive splits top down. Point
    assignment keeps a live set of clusters and lets points
    move between them.
  - k-means: choose k, place k centers, assign each point to
    its nearest center, move each center to the mean of its
    points (the centroid, the center of mass), repeat until
    assignments stop changing.
  - Initialization matters: random points in space (expired),
    random points from the data (wired, the standard), or
    k-means++ (upgraded), which spreads the centers apart.
  - Simple and automatic, but you supply k and every point is
    forced into a cluster, which makes it too sensitive to
    outliers.

10. QUIZ-SAFE FACTS
  - Clustering discovers groups and structures in the data
    that are similar to each other; members of a cluster
    should be more alike among each other than to members of
    other clusters. It is unsupervised learning.
  - Hierarchical clustering has two subcategories:
    agglomerative (bottom up, each point starts as its own
    cluster, the two nearest are repeatedly combined) and
    divisive (top down, one cluster recursively split).
  - Point assignment algorithms are the more popular type:
    they maintain a set of clusters, points belong to the
    nearest one, and that can change until convergence.
  - The three questions of hierarchical clustering: how to
    represent a cluster of more than one point, how to
    determine nearness, when to stop combining clusters. The
    answers: the centroid, the average of all its data points
    and the center of mass; the distance between centroids;
    and one cluster for agglomerative, all single points for
    divisive.
  - k-means is the most popular clustering algorithm, is
    popular because it is very simple, and works with
    numerical data only. k must be specified before running.
  - The k-means steps: pick k cluster centers at random,
    assign every point to its nearest center (for example by
    Euclidean distance), move each center to the new mean of
    its assigned items, repeat until convergence, which is
    when the change in cluster assignment is less than a
    threshold, ideally zero. A timeout parameter may stop the
    run after a number of iterations.
  - In the lecture's eight point example, two random
    centroids split the points three green and five blue on
    the first pass, one green point that had been blue was
    reassigned after the centroids moved, and the run
    converged with four green and four blue.
  - The assignment shortcut: a dashed line perpendicular to
    the line connecting two centroids, through its middle,
    separates the points closer to one from the rest.
  - The assignment of the initial centroids is very crucial.
    The professor's three labels: expired (completely random
    points in the point space, not really used), wired (k
    random points from the existing set of points, the
    standard k-means approach), upgraded (k-means++, which
    picks one random point then chooses the rest from a
    distribution favoring points farther from those already
    picked, and is shown to be a little better).
  - Advantages of k-means: simple, very easy to understand,
    automatically assigns items to clusters. Disadvantages:
    you must pick k beforehand, all items are forced into
    clusters, and it is too sensitive to outliers.
