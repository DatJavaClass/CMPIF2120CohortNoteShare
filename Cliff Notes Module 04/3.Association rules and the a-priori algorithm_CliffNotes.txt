1. WHAT AN ASSOCIATION RULE IS
  - Association rules describe the patterns found in the data,
    an intuitive way to let decision makers make sense of what
    gets discovered.
  - KEY TERM: association rule: a rule R with an itemset on the
    left hand side and an itemset on the right hand side. The
    left hand side, the set of products someone buys, implies
    the right hand side, another set of products they also buy.
  - Two conditions on the form. The two itemsets are disjoint,
    so they have no items in common, and itemset 2 is non
    empty, so the right hand side cannot be the empty set.
  - KEY TERM: disjoint: sharing no member. An item cannot stand
    on both sides of the same rule.
  - Some simplified definitions allow only one item on the
    right hand side. This class does not use that restriction.
  - What the rule claims: if a transaction includes itemset 1,
    then it also has itemset 2. The lecture's example forms are
    A, B implies E and A implies B, C.
  - Running story for the worked examples here: a bagel cart
    outside the library keeps one line per customer, the items
    in the bag and nothing else. Six items, shortened to
    letters the way the lecture does: A = bagel, B = coffee,
    C = cream cheese, D = orange juice, E = muffin, F = tea.
    Ten receipts in the log. (added)
  - Note 2 of this module found the frequent sets in that log
    at min_sup = 3 of 10 receipts: singles A, B, C, D, E, pairs
    AB, AC, BC, BD, BE, and the triple ABC (F is not frequent,
    count 1). Those eleven sets feed every rule below. (added)

2. GENERATING EVERY RULE FROM A FREQUENT SET
  - Going from frequent itemsets to association rules is
    relatively simple: generate all possible rules. Identify
    every possible split point of the set into a left hand side
    and a right hand side, with the caveat that the order of
    the items inside a set does not matter.
  - The lecture works the splits by shape. Two items on the
    left and one on the right are trivially identified: pick
    one item for the right, and everything that remains goes on
    the left. Then one on the left and two on the right, built
    the same way with the sides reversed.
  - The empty left hand side is technically available. The
    lecture raises it one time to show it does not make much
    sense, then ignores it from that point on.
  - A set of n items has 2^n - 2 splits: every item goes left
    or right, and the two arrangements that put everything on
    one side are thrown out. Three items give six rules. (added
    explanation)
  - On the cart, ABC is the only frequent triple. Its six
    rules:

      AB -> C    AC -> B    BC -> A
      A -> BC    B -> AC    C -> AB

3. SUPPORT AND CONFIDENCE OF A RULE
  - With so many rules generated, two metrics decide which are
    meaningful and which are not.
  - KEY TERM: support of an association rule: for a rule saying
    itemset I implies itemset J, the support of the union of
    the two itemsets. Support count and support percentage
    carry over from itemsets to rules unchanged.
  - What rule support means: what fraction of transactions
    involve both itemsets that form the rule, that is, whether
    the rule has enough samples in the data set to merit
    looking into the pattern. Something very rare is probably
    not worth pursuing even when it is real.
  - Every rule generated from the same frequent set has the
    exact same support at the rule level, since wherever the
    split falls the union of the two parts is the original set
    again. If one qualifies on minimum support, all of them do,
    so rules drawn from a frequent set never need that
    computation at all.
  - KEY TERM: confidence of an association rule: the support of
    the entire rule, the left hand side joined with the right
    hand side, over the support of just the left hand side. It
    reads as the probability that the right hand side appears
    given that the left hand side already appears.
  - Confidence does vary across the rules from one frequent
    set: the numerator stays fixed while the denominator, the
    left hand side, changes with every split. (added
    explanation)
  - KEY TERM: minimum confidence (min_conf): the confidence a
    rule has to reach to be kept, a threshold handed to the
    algorithm the same way min_sup is. (added definition)
  - Read together: support says whether a pattern is popular,
    and confidence says whether there is a good conversion rate
    in the data.

4. THE SIX RULES FROM ABC, SCORED
  - Thresholds for the cart: min_sup = 3 transactions = 3/10 =
    30%, and min_conf = 70%. Both are given numbers, the way
    the lecture describes someone handing them over. (added)
  - ABC has support count 3, which is 30%, from receipts T1, T2
    and T6. That is the rule support for all six splits and it
    clears min_sup, so the support test is settled for the
    whole batch before a single confidence gets computed.
  - Confidence is sup(ABC) over the support of whatever sits on
    the left:

      two on the left, one on the right
      AB -> C   3/5 = 60.0%   fail
      AC -> B   3/4 = 75.0%   pass
      BC -> A   3/4 = 75.0%   pass
      one on the left, two on the right
      A -> BC   3/6 = 50.0%   fail
      B -> AC   3/8 = 37.5%   fail
      C -> AB   3/6 = 50.0%   fail

  - Two of the six survive at 70%: AC -> B, bagel and cream
    cheese then coffee, and BC -> A, coffee and cream cheese
    then bagel.
  - The empty left hand side rule: {} -> ABC has confidence
    sup(ABC) over 10 = 3/10 = 30%, since every receipt contains
    the empty set. It states no condition, so it is never
    useful.

5. STRONG RULES ACROSS THE WHOLE CART
  - KEY TERM: strong association rule: a rule with at least the
    given minimum support and at least the given minimum
    confidence. Identifying those is the stated goal of the a
    priori algorithm.
  - Rules come out of every frequent set of size 2 or more. On
    the cart that is the five pairs at two rules each plus the
    six from ABC, sixteen in all. The pair rules score like
    this:

      A -> B    5/6 = 83.3%   pass
      B -> A    5/8 = 62.5%   fail
      A -> C    4/6 = 66.7%   fail
      C -> A    4/6 = 66.7%   fail
      B -> C    4/8 = 50.0%   fail
      C -> B    4/6 = 66.7%   fail
      B -> D    3/8 = 37.5%   fail
      D -> B    3/3 = 100%    pass
      B -> E    3/8 = 37.5%   fail
      E -> B    3/3 = 100%    pass

  - The strong rules, the five that clear both thresholds,
    ordered by confidence:

      D -> B   100%    (sup 3)
      E -> B   100%    (sup 3)
      A -> B   83.3%   (sup 5)
      AC -> B  75%     (sup 3)
      BC -> A  75%     (sup 3)

  - Reading the list: coffee sits in 8 of the 10 bags, so
    almost anything implies coffee. The reverse rules fail
    because coffee alone does not say what else was bought:
    B -> D and B -> E land at 37.5%.

6. THE A PRIORI ALGORITHM
  - KEY TERM: a priori algorithm: the efficient procedure for
    finding the patterns behind association rules, by first
    identifying the frequent itemsets and only then generating
    rules from them.
  - The start is easy: find the size 1 itemsets, count the
    frequency of every item, and keep the ones above the
    threshold. The trick is the mechanism that moves from k to
    k plus 1.
  - That mechanism is the subset property from note 2: every
    subset of a frequent itemset is itself frequent. Used in
    reverse it prunes out sets with no chance. To move up a
    level, all possible subsets of the bigger itemset have to
    be frequent themselves, and one that is not vetoes the
    bigger set.
  - KEY TERM: candidate itemset: a size k set the join step
    proposed and the prune step did not veto, still to be
    counted against the data before anyone knows whether it is
    frequent. (added definition)
  - The pseudocode:

      init: take the min_sup parameter and the min_conf
        parameter, and generate all frequent itemsets of
        size 1.
      step 2a: generate the size k candidates using the size
        k minus 1 sets. Join: combine the size k minus 1
        itemsets to create size k sets. Prune: remove any
        candidate that has a subset one level down which is
        not in the frequent list.
      step 2b: count. For the candidates that survived the
        prune, go to the data set and count how many times
        each size k itemset shows up.
      step 2c: prune again on the actual counts, keeping the
        sets that reach min_sup. The rest were only
        candidates and never made the frequent list.
      repeat: loop for the next size k.
      stop: when step 2a generates no candidate itemsets.
      then: generate the association rules by enumerating
        the splits of the frequent itemsets, and using the
        confidence metric keep those at or above min_conf.

  - Going from size 1 to size 2 makes the prune concrete: build
    all possible size 2 itemsets, then eliminate every one
    containing an item that is not frequent.
  - Checking a candidate of size k means checking every subset
    of size k minus 1, and those are easy to find: hide one of
    the letters and look at what remains, k of them per
    candidate. The itemsets are kept in lexicographic order,
    which improves the efficiency of doing this and is
    generally good practice algorithmically.
  - If any one subset is missing from the frequent list, the
    candidate is dead. The lecture's phrase is ultimate veto
    power: only one is enough to block the larger set from
    becoming a candidate itemset.

7. A PRIORI ON THE CART, START TO FINISH
  - Init: min_sup = 3, min_conf = 70%, and every single item is
    counted. A 6, B 8, C 6, D 3, E 3, F 1. L1 = {A, B, C, D,
    E}, F dropping out at count 1.
  - k = 2, step 2a join: pair up the members of L1. The
    candidates C2 are the 10 pairs among A, B, C, D and E,
    since 5 choose 2 = 10. The five pairs containing F are
    never built: 15 pairs exist among six items, 10 become
    candidates. The prune has nothing left to do here, since
    every size 1 subset of every candidate is in L1 by
    construction.
  - Step 2b count: AB 5, AC 4, AD 2, AE 2, BC 4, BD 3, BE 3,
    CD 1, CE 1, DE 0.
  - Step 2c: L2 = {AB, AC, BC, BD, BE}. The counts eliminated
    the candidates AD, AE, CD, CE and DE.
  - k = 3, step 2a join: combine the pairs in L2 that share
    their first item, that is k minus 2 = 1 item in common,
    reading in lexicographic order:

      AB + AC -> ABC
      BC + BD -> BCD
      BC + BE -> BCE
      BD + BE -> BDE

  - Prune, hiding one item at a time and looking for what
    remains in L2:

      ABC: hide A -> BC ok, hide B -> AC ok,
           hide C -> AB ok.    candidate
      BCD: hide B -> CD missing (count 1).    vetoed
      BCE: hide B -> CE missing (count 1).    vetoed
      BDE: hide B -> DE missing (count 0).    vetoed

  - Three of the four proposals die on a lookup, so only ABC
    reaches the receipts. Step 2b count: ABC = 3. Step 2c:
    L3 = {ABC}.
  - k = 4: L3 holds a single set, so there is nothing to join
    it with, no C4 gets produced, and the loop stops.
  - Frequent itemsets found: A, B, C, D, E, AB, AC, BC, BD, BE,
    ABC. That is the same list a full enumeration gives, but
    the algorithm counted 10 pairs and 1 triple against the
    receipts instead of 15 pairs and 20 triples.
  - Then the rule step on those sets, which is section 5,
    ending at the five strong rules.

8. WHY PRUNE: THE COMBINATORICS
  - Pruning helps because these are all combinatorial
    mechanisms, and the numbers get out of hand very quickly.
  - The number of all possible size k itemsets out of n
    products, when there are no repetitions and order does not
    matter, is n choose k:

      n choose k = n! / (k! (n - k)!)

    where n! is the factorial of n: 5! is 5 times 4 times 3
    times 2 times 1.
  - On the cart's six items:

      size 1:  6 choose 1 = 6
      size 2:  6 choose 2 = 15
      size 3:  6 choose 3 = 720 / (6 * 6) = 20

  - F is not frequent, so every set containing F is vetoed
    before it is ever counted, and only five items combine:

      size 2:  5 choose 2 = 10   (15 drops to 10)
      size 3:  5 choose 3 = 10   (20 drops to 10)

  - The size 2 results narrow it again: once L2 is known, only
    ABC needs counting. On six items that saving looks modest,
    on thousands of products it decides whether the job
    finishes. (added note)

9. STORING THE PAIR COUNTS
  - An implementation detail for real life: how support counts
    get stored. The items are not held as strings, but mapped
    to integers first.
  - Three ways to hold the pair counts:
      A plain matrix for the pair i j. The naive method, and
        it stores every pair twice.
      A triangular method, which does a little arithmetic on
        the index so that double the data does not have to be
        stored.
      A triples approach, a hash table using the pair to be
        counted as the key.
  - On the cart's six items:
      plain matrix: 6 * 6 = 36 cells, each pair stored twice,
        plus a diagonal nobody needs.
      triangular: each unordered pair once, 6 * 5 / 2 = 15
        cells.
      triples: only the pairs seen at least once, 10 nonzero
        pairs at 3 numbers each (item, item, count) = 30
        numbers.
  - Which method wins depends on how many of the 15 pairs are
    actually nonzero: triples pays nothing for pairs that never
    occurred, so it wins on sparse data. (added explanation)

10. REAL DATA BEYOND A FIXED PRODUCT LIST
  - With real data there is normally no fixed notion of what a
    product is. Usually there is a hierarchy: a particular
    brand of low fat milk, or low fat milk as the product type,
    or milk, or drinks, and the question is whether
    associations at any of those levels are useful.
  - Other things that make matters more complicated: sequences
    over time, and patterns that change over time.

11. KEY TAKEAWAYS
  - A rule is a split of a frequent itemset: both sides
    disjoint, right side non empty, every split enumerated,
    2^n - 2 of them for n items.
  - Support belongs to the set, not to the split. All six rules
    out of ABC carry support 3, so rules built from frequent
    sets never need their support checked.
  - Confidence is where the split matters, since only the
    denominator moves. The rarer the left hand side, the higher
    it climbs: AC -> B passes at 75% while B -> AC fails at
    37.5% off the same numerator.
  - Strong means both thresholds at once. Sixteen rules on the
    cart yield five strong ones, four of them ending in coffee,
    which is in 8 of the 10 bags.
  - A priori spends a lookup to avoid a scan: the join builds
    candidates, the prune kills any missing a subset one level
    down, and only the survivors get counted.
  - The savings are combinatorial. Dropping one infrequent item
    out of six cut the pairs to count from 15 to 10, and the
    size 2 results cut the triples reaching the counting step
    from 20 to 1.

12. QUIZ-SAFE FACTS
  - An association rule R has an itemset on the left hand side
    implying an itemset on the right hand side.
  - The two itemsets are disjoint, and itemset 2 is non empty.
  - Some simplified definitions restrict itemset 2 to a single
    item; this class does not use that restriction.
  - The rule means that if a transaction includes itemset 1, it
    also has itemset 2. The example forms given are A, B
    implies E and A implies B, C.
  - Rules are generated from a frequent set by identifying all
    possible split points into a left hand side and a right
    hand side, without regard to item order.
  - The right hand side cannot be empty. The empty left hand
    side is technically possible, is mentioned once to show it
    is not useful, then ignored.
  - The support of the rule I implies J is the support of the
    union of the two itemsets, with a count form and a
    percentage form.
  - Rule support asks what fraction of transactions involve
    both sides, that is, whether the rule has enough samples to
    merit looking into the pattern.
  - All rules from the same frequent set have the exact same
    support at the rule level, because wherever the split falls
    the union of the two parts is the original set. If one
    qualifies on minimum support, all of them do.
  - Confidence is the support of the union of both sides over
    the support of the left hand side alone, read as the
    probability that the right hand side appears given the left
    hand side already appears.
  - The lecture's table gives the rule A, B implies C a
    confidence of support A, B, C over support A, B, which is
    2 over 4, or 50%.
  - From the lecture's frequent set A, B, E at a minimum
    support of 2 and a minimum confidence of 50%: AB -> E is
    2/4 or 50%, AE -> B is 2/2, BE -> A is 2/2 and E -> AB is
    2/2, all four qualifying.
  - From that same set A -> BE is 2/6, about 33%, and B -> AE
    is 2/7, about 28%, both failing.
  - The empty left hand side rule there has confidence 2/9,
    since all nine transactions contain the empty set.
  - Support figures out whether a pattern is popular,
    confidence whether there is a good conversion rate in the
    data.
  - Strong association rules are the ones that have at least
    the given minimum support and the given minimum confidence.
  - A priori initialization: take the minimum support and
    minimum confidence parameters and generate the frequent
    itemsets of size 1.
  - Step 2a generates the size k candidates from the size k
    minus 1 sets: a join step, then a prune step that removes
    any candidate with a subset below it that is not frequent.
  - Going from size 1 to size 2, any size 2 itemset containing
    an item that is not frequent is eliminated.
  - Step 2b counts the survivors in the data set, and step 2c
    keeps the ones that are actually frequent.
  - The loop repeats until 2a generates no candidates. Then the
    rules are generated and those at or above the minimum
    confidence kept.
  - The subsets of size k minus 1 are found by hiding one of
    the letters at a time.
  - The itemsets are written in lexicographic order to improve
    efficiency, algorithmically as well.
  - The lecture's example assumes five frequent size 3
    itemsets: ABC, ABD, ACD, ACE and BCD.
  - ABCD is a good candidate, because hiding each letter in
    turn gives BCD, ACD, ABD and ABC, all present in the list.
  - ACDE cannot be frequent: CDE does not appear in the list,
    and ADE does not either, even though ACD and ACE both do.
    One missing subset is enough, what the lecture calls
    ultimate veto power.
  - For a very small data set of seven products, the lecture
    counts 7 size 1 itemsets, 21 size 2 and 35 size 3, with
    7 choose 3 working out to 35.
  - The number of all possible size k itemsets out of n
    products, without repetitions and without regard to order,
    is given by n choose k, which is n! / (k! (n - k)!), where
    n! is the factorial of n.
  - In the lecture's reduction, if D is not frequent every size
    2 itemset containing D is vetoed, and with F and Z not
    frequent either the 21 pairs drop to 6 that need counting.
  - Support counts are not stored with strings; the items are
    mapped to integers.
  - Three storage approaches are named: a plain matrix for the
    pair i j, the naive method, a triangular method that avoids
    storing double the data, and a triples approach using a
    hash table keyed by the pair to be counted.
  - Real data normally has no fixed notion for the products.
    Often there is a hierarchy: a particular brand of low fat
    milk, low fat milk, milk, drinks, with associations
    possible at any level.
  - Sequences over time and patterns that change over time
    complicate matters further.
