1. WHAT ASSOCIATION RULE MINING IS
  - KEY TERM: market basket data: a list of the items people
    buy at the grocery store.
  - KEY TERM: association rule mining: the technique that works
    on market basket data to discover interesting and novel
    patterns in it.
  - KEY TERM: pattern: a combination of items that turns up
    together across enough baskets to be worth acting on. The
    lecture's bar is the word novel: not obvious, but usable.
    (added definition)
  - KEY TERM: correlation: two items bought together more
    often than chance would suggest. The lecture uses it and
    pattern for the same finding. (added definition)
  - Retailers collect this data aggressively to get insights
    about customers and use those insights to increase profits.
  - Running story for the worked examples here: a bagel cart
    outside the library keeps one line per customer, the items
    in the bag and nothing else. Ten such lines are market
    basket data. (added)

2. THE DIAPERS AND BEER STORY
  - Back in the mid 90s, when association rule mining became
    popular, a strong correlation was discovered between people
    buying diapers and beer on the same grocery trip, usually
    between 5 and 7 p.m. on Fridays.
  - Several news stories and Wikipedia entries cover it, and
    the original article is below the video. In the article,
    people analyzing data at some stores found that on Friday
    nights those who came in to buy diapers also often bought
    beer.
  - The purchases were attributed to new fathers who do not
    often go out on Saturday nights: stuck home with babies
    over the weekend, maybe you drink a few beers at home
    instead of going out. That reason was laid over the pattern
    afterward, since the data gave the pairing and the hours,
    not the why. (added last sentence)

3. WHY THE CORRELATION IS INVISIBLE WITHOUT DATA
  - The important thing to understand is that the correlation
    is not easily observable without data. It is not as if
    every Friday night you could go to a store and see carts
    loaded up with diapers and beer.
  - Instead, association rule mining looks for correlations
    between items even if it is a low percentage of the overall
    customer population.

4. ACTING ON A SMALL PATTERN
  - The store can use that knowledge to adjust other things:
    putting chips between the diapers and the beer, or offering
    coupons for chips with the purchase of beer, to encourage
    other spending.
  - While 1% of all customers is a small number, influencing 1%
    of a huge number of customers will cover the salary of the
    data scientists who conducted the analysis.

5. GENERALIZING THE TYPES OF PRODUCTS
  - One complication with real life settings is that we often
    need to generalize the types of products to get more
    meaningful patterns.
  - There may be no pattern with organic 2% milk of a specific
    brand, but there can be one with milk in general: that
    people buy milk and cereal together. Focus on specific
    brands and such a pattern may not be popular enough, so it
    could stay undetected. The mechanism is division: one real
    pattern split across a dozen brands becomes a dozen
    patterns, none big enough to clear the threshold. (added
    explanation)

6. LOYALTY CARDS AS DATA COLLECTION
  - Every supermarket now has a loyalty card. The cards were
    once a novelty; they are mainstream nowadays.
  - The main reason they exist is to gather data for machine
    learning, association mining being the lecture's example.
    The card links you or your household to a specific ID
    number that can be used for data mining and that informs
    business decision making.
  - At first you could get the store specials without a card.
    Nowadays it is necessary for any special pricing, to force
    people into getting and using a card and to increase the
    quality of the resulting data mining.

7. KEY TAKEAWAYS
  - Association rule mining runs on market basket data and
    hunts for patterns that are novel rather than obvious.
  - The lesson of diapers and beer is that the pattern was
    invisible from the sales floor. Only the data showed it.
  - A pattern covering few customers still pays, because the
    payoff scales with the size of the customer base, and it is
    acted on by placing or pricing a third product.
  - Product descriptions that are too specific hide patterns,
    and loyalty cards exist to collect the data in the first
    place.
